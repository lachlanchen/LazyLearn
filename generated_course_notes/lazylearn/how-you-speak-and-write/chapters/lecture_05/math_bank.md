## Definitions And Objects
- `writing space`: any repeatable place where the work can actually happen; need not be ideal.
- `writing tools`: the minimal physical setup that keeps drafting uninterrupted, especially computer, chair, and nearby materials.
- `big idea`: a concept large enough to sustain book length rather than collapsing into article-scale treatment.
- `outline`: a directional scaffold for the manuscript; minimal for some fiction, more explicit for nonfiction.
- `Marathon of the Middle`: the dangerous middle stretch, roughly around the halfway to three-quarter region, where momentum and confidence sag.
- `setup`: an early planted premise, question, difficulty, or expectation.
- `payoff`: the later fulfillment, answer, solution, or consequence of the setup.
- `daily quota`: computed writing target per day from total goal and available time.
- `research`: background knowledge used to create accuracy, specificity, and credibility.
- `reader-first filter`: the rule that manuscript decisions are judged primarily by reader impact.
- `conflict`: overt disagreement or friction on the page.
- `tension`: unresolved pressure, implication, or expectancy that keeps the reader moving.
- `internal editor`: the perfectionist critic that should be suppressed during the first draft.
- `resounding ending`: an ending with strong closure and emotional force.
- `ferocious self-editor`: the later-stage reviser who cuts aggressively and polishes every word.

## Derivation Steps
Project-scale reduction:
1. Start from the felt size of a whole book.
2. Replace whole-book thinking with smaller units.
3. Resolve those units into chapters, paragraphs, and sentences.
4. Work one unit at a time.
5. Therefore the colossal task becomes operationally manageable.

Book-worthiness test:
1. Ask whether the concept is truly book-scale.
2. Compare it against article or blog scale.
3. Retell the idea to another person and watch whether it expands.
4. If it repeatedly stalls early, it is probably too small.
5. If it keeps growing, it has the legs for a book.

Why outlining survives even for pantsers:
1. Admit that some fiction writers draft by discovery.
2. Note that even discovery writing still requires directional awareness.
3. Increase outline detail when editors or agents require a synopsis.
4. Require explicit outline structure for nonfiction.
5. Conclude that outline degree varies, but the structural function remains.

Middle-structure logic:
1. The middle is where boredom and padding enter.
2. Save meaningful setups and payoffs for the middle.
3. In nonfiction, stage the problem so the solution matters when it arrives.
4. In fiction, preserve pressure, difficulty, and delayed consequence.
5. Therefore the middle must be designed to thrive, not merely endured.

Schedule computation:
1. Estimate total page count or word count.
2. Estimate the number of writing days.
3. Divide total target by available days.
4. Compare the result with actual daily capacity.
5. Adjust schedule or deadline once if needed.
6. Treat the final deadline as fixed.

Research discipline:
1. Gather enough research to produce specificity and credibility.
2. Recognize the temptation to display all of it.
3. Keep the story or argument as the main course.
4. Use research only where it sharpens believability.
5. Therefore research functions as support, not as the book’s substance.

Reader-first propulsion:
1. Build an opener that creates immediate curiosity.
2. Pass later decisions through the reader-first filter.
3. Maintain unresolved pressure on the page.
4. In fiction, pressure often appears as conflict or relational strain.
5. In nonfiction, pressure often appears as promise followed by later delivery.

Draft-to-revision sequence:
1. Silence the internal editor during drafting.
2. Get the story or argument down first.
3. Wait before revising.
4. Return with a perfectionist stance only after the draft exists.
5. Cut, tighten, and strengthen.
6. Select the ending with the greatest emotional force.
