## Definitions And Objects

- \(T\): time spent writing, in the narrow literal sense used in the opening contrast.
- \(C\): total cost of writing, meant rhetorically to include emotional and intellectual expenditure, not a measurable scalar.
- \(E\): emotional capability engaged in the act of writing.
- \(I\): intellectual capability engaged in the act of writing.
- \(L\): long-incubated idea, image, character, moment, or experience.
- \(X\): activating collision with an event, another text, or a lived experience.
- \(U,D,G,W\): urgency, density, gravity, and weight as the qualities that make an idea become story-worthy.
- Story engine: the local three-part object \((\text{character},\text{want},\text{obstacle})\).
- Narrative orientation: the stabilizing effect of knowing a beginning, ending, or first line before the middle is clear.
- Character-driven / plot-driven: not two disjoint genres here, but two poles of a spectrum used to diagnose imbalance.
- Soul of the writer: an added axis beyond character and plot, covering obsession, place, fear, desire, bodily memory, and recurring inward questions.
- Sensory memory: bodily knowledge used to enter fictional situations not literally lived by the writer.
- Failure: not final collapse, but productive iteration inside the drafting loop.
- Honeymoon read: first reading with judgment suspended.
- Seven-year itch: second reading with analytic and critical attention restored.
- Vitality / non-deadness: the criterion by which readers and writers tell that the story is alive on the page.
- Estrangement: the distance needed for the writer to become a colder reader of the work.
- Bullshit detector: the practical test triggered by reading aloud or editorial distance.
- Editor: external intelligence that marks where life is missing, weak, or overclaimed.

## Derivation Steps

1. Opening emotional criterion.
1. The lecture starts from readerly effect.
2. It claims that emotional effect cannot be generated by emotionally neutral writing.
3. It strengthens the claim by saying the work must cost more than time alone.
4. It then identifies the working state as one in which emotional and intellectual capacities are both active.

2. Story-origin chain.
1. A writer may carry an idea for a long time without yet having a story.
2. A later encounter, event, or reading collides with that idea.
3. That collision produces urgency, density, gravity, and weight.
4. Only then does the material become story-ready.
5. In some accounts, the story still does not truly exist until actual writing begins.

3. Character engine.
1. Begin with a particular character.
2. Ask what that character wants.
3. Ask what blocks that want.
4. Treat the resulting pressure as the engine of narrative movement.

4. Aristotle-to-modern balance.
1. One classical pole subordinates character to action.
2. The modern discussion loosens that subordination.
3. Too much plot pressure yields arbitrary twists and mechanical movement.
4. Too much character without pressure yields drift and reader indifference.
5. The usable model is a balance, not an absolute victory of one side.

5. Soul/self extension.
1. Character and plot do not exhaust what drives fiction.
2. Writers repeatedly return to place, obsession, fear, and recurring inward questions.
3. Therefore even remote fiction can remain deeply personal.
4. Bodily and sensory memory provide the bridge from self to distant world.

6. Failure loop.
1. Drafting produces imperfect material.
2. Imperfection is not merely tolerated but used.
3. Repeated restart or redraft exposes the limits of the writer’s current attempt.
4. Through repetition the story begins to disclose its own demands.
5. Revision then shifts from pure generation to shaped craft.

7. Reader-awareness principle.
1. Early drafting is private and heat-driven.
2. Revision introduces the presence of another reader.
3. The beginning therefore does double duty: it starts the story and trains the reader how to read it.
4. This is why beginnings are often slow and disproportionately important.

8. Reading model.
1. First reading suspends judgment and lets the work act on us.
2. Second reading returns analytically to marked passages and emerging argument.
3. The lecture names these two passes explicitly.
4. This two-pass model then feeds back into workshop practice and criticism.

9. Vitality test.
1. A good story is not defined first by genre or formal package.
2. It is defined by vitality, liveliness, non-deadness, and the power to draw us in.
3. Writers also detect this internally as a felt vibration when the work comes close.
4. Reading aloud and editorial pressure are then used to test whether that life is really present.

10. Editing refinement.
1. “Kill your darlings” is presented as a useful discipline.
2. The lecture then checks that slogan by warning against automatic cutting.
3. The better question is whether the difficult passage is bad or merely not yet well written.
4. Hence good editing identifies where life is thin and demands stronger writing, not just deletion.

## Notation Choices

- Use \(\Rightarrow\) for directional dependence, causal implication, or practical necessity asserted by the lecture.
- Use \(\to\) for temporal or process sequence inside drafting, reading, or revision loops.
- Use \(\leftrightarrow\) for a live spectrum or bidirectional tension, especially character-driven versus plot-driven.
- Use \(\not\Rightarrow\) only for the autobiographical point, where the lecture explicitly rejects a naive inference.
- Prefer roman text objects, e.g. \(\text{character}\), \(\text{action}\), \(\text{vitality}\), rather than abstract variables, unless the variables help compress a repeatedly used relation.
- Keep \(C,T,E,I,L,X,U,D,G,W\) only as local abbreviations in working notes; expand them in finished prose unless a displayed equation genuinely helps.
- Treat “story engine” as the triple \((\text{character},\text{want},\text{obstacle})\), not as a symbolic operator.
- Treat “vitality,” “non-deadness,” and “being drawn in” as near-equivalent labels inside one criterion, not as formally distinct observables.
- Do not introduce calculus, probability, optimization, or geometric axes unless the final chapter absolutely needs a tiny schematic; the lecture itself does not supply that machinery.
- Because there are no validated frames, do not label anything as board notation or slide notation.

## Uncertain Mathematics

- There is no [visible] mathematics for this lecture. All formal relations are transcript-backed or editorial reconstruction.
- The inequality \(C>T\) is rhetorical, not quantitative. The final chapter should not pretend that cost is measurable.
- The relation \(\text{working story} \Rightarrow E+I\) is also rhetorical shorthand for “all cylinders are firing,” not an additive law.
- \(\text{character} \prec \text{action}\) is a compressed notation for the panel’s description of Aristotle; it should be attributed carefully and not overstated as a direct quotation from the Poetics.
- \(\text{character-driven} \leftrightarrow \text{plot-driven}\) is best treated as a spectrum, not a one-dimensional numerical axis, unless the prose later explicitly motivates such a simplification.
- “Soul of the writer,” “prismatic hologram,” “nuclear fusion,” and “vibrational thing” are metaphor-heavy. They can motivate note equations, but the final chapter should mark them as conceptual metaphors rather than literal theory.
- The “failure loop” is supported strongly by process testimony, but its exact cyclic form is editorial packaging.
- “Edit well \neq cut mechanically” is a valid working shorthand, but it is not stated in symbolic form in the lecture.
- Proper nouns in garbled transcript segments should not be used to ground any formal claim.
