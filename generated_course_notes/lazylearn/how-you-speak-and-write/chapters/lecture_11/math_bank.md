## Definitions And Objects
- \(Q\): overall quality or effectiveness of speaking/writing.
- \(K\): knowledge.
- \(P\): practice.
- \(T\): inherent talent.
- One-language processor: Winston’s claim that audience members cannot simultaneously read dense text and listen well.
- Empowerment promise: an opening promise about what the audience will know or be able to do by the end.
- Cycle: repeated passes around the same subject.
- Build a fence: distinguish one’s idea from nearby but different ideas.
- Verbal punctuation: explicit landmarks, numbering, and recap points that let listeners “get back on the bus.”
- Question: a deliberate audience prompt used to re-engage attention.
- Time and place objects: \(11\,\mathrm{a.m.}\), well lit, cased, reasonably populated.
- Boards / props / slides:
  - board for informing and teaching
  - slides for exposing ideas
  - props for memorable embodied demonstrations
- Bicycle-wheel object: spinning wheel with a marked rim segment under duct tape.
- Pendulum object: heavy steel ball released from rest at nose height.
- Winston’s Star: symbol, slogan, surprise, salient idea, story.
- Near miss: an almost-example that teaches the decisive structural feature.
- One-shot learning: learning something definite from a single example.

## Derivation Steps
Communication formula to anti-talent conclusion
1. State that speaking and writing quality depend on knowledge, practice, and talent.
2. Use the skiing anecdote to compare high talent with low task-specific knowledge and practice.
3. Observe that Winston skis better because he has the relevant \(K\) and \(P\), while Mary Lou Retton mainly brings \(T\).
4. Conclude that \(K\) and \(P\) can dominate \(T\).
5. Infer that talent should be treated as the small term in the formula.

Cycling / repetition heuristic
1. Assume that at any given moment about \(20\%\) of listeners are fogged out.
2. A single pass through an idea therefore leaves some fraction behind.
3. Repeating the same point from multiple angles increases the chance that the full room catches it.
4. Hence the advice to cycle around the subject rather than say it only once.

Question timing heuristic
1. Asking a question is another way to bring people back onto the bus.
2. The speaker experiences silence as longer than the audience does.
3. Winston’s practical rule is to wait about seven seconds.
4. The question must be neither too obvious nor too hard.

Wheel-direction argument
1. Start with a spinning bicycle wheel and pose the directional puzzle.
2. Ignore memorized hand rules as the primary method.
3. Mark a small rim segment with duct tape.
4. Focus on that local segment rather than on the wheel as a whole.
5. Let the marked segment come over the top.
6. Apply the puff or effective push to that local piece.
7. Infer the direction that this piece now wants to move.
8. Repeat the same local reasoning for the next segment.
9. Conclude that the wheel as a whole must go in that direction.

Pendulum energy demonstration
1. Lift the steel ball to nose height and release it from rest.
2. Convert gravitational potential into kinetic energy on the downswing.
3. Convert kinetic back into potential on the return.
4. If one truly only lets go, the return height cannot exceed the release height.
5. Therefore the ball returns to kiss the nose rather than crash through it.
6. The real danger is not the law of energy conservation but the human tendency to push.

Slide-overload argument
1. Dense slides recruit the audience’s language processor into reading.
2. Once reading dominates, listening quality falls.
3. Experimental anecdote: students remember what was on the slide better than what was spoken.
4. Therefore reduce words, clutter, logos, and unnecessary titles.

Job-talk framework
1. A job candidate must establish both vision and evidence of accomplishment.
2. Winston’s timing claim is that this must happen in roughly five minutes.
3. Vision is framed as an important problem plus a distinctive approach.
4. “Done something” is framed as concrete steps toward solution plus some implemented or demonstrated result.
5. The ending mirrors those steps as contributions.

Recognition / memory framework
1. Attach a symbol to the work.
2. Attach a slogan or handle.
3. Include a surprise.
4. Make one salient idea stick out.
5. Tell the story of how it works and why it matters.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(Q\) for communication quality.
- Use \(K\), \(P\), \(T\) only in the opening communication formula:
  - \(K\) = knowledge
  - \(P\) = practice
  - \(T\) = talent
- In the pendulum / energy example, avoid reusing \(P\) for potential energy; use \(K_{\mathrm{kin}}\) and \(V_{\mathrm{pot}}\).
- Use \(p_{\mathrm{fog}}\) for the \(20\%\) fog-out heuristic.
- Use \(t_{\mathrm{wait}}\) for the seven-second pause heuristic.
- Use \(p_{\mathrm{guess\ correct}}\) for the wheel hand-rule anecdote.
- Treat \(\succ\) in the ranking \(\text{speaking} \succ \text{writing} \succ \text{quality of ideas}\) as editorial shorthand, not a formal operator from the lecture.
- Keep Winston’s Star as a prose list, not as an invented symbolic object.
- Do not introduce \(\tau\), \(\mathbf L\), \(\Omega\), or precession notation unless explicitly marked as later editorial physics supplementation.
- Keep “one-language processor,” “empowerment promise,” “build a fence,” and “verbal punctuation” as prose terms, not formal symbols.
