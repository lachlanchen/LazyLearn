## Definitions And Objects

- Human voice: the universal instrument “we all play.”
- Listenability: the listener’s willingness and ability to attend to a speaker.
- \(S_7\): the ordered repertoire of seven deadly sins of speaking.
- Gossip: speaking ill of someone not present.
- Judging: speaking from a stance that makes the listener feel assessed and found wanting.
- Negativity: sustained hostile or pessimistic framing.
- Complaining: treated in the lecture as a distinct habit, though introduced as another form of negativity.
- Excuses: blame-shifting and refusal of responsibility.
- Embroidery: exaggeration; inflation of language that can slide into lying.
- Dogmatism: confusion of facts with opinions.
- \(\mathrm{HAIL}\): positive four-part foundation for powerful speech.
- Honesty: being true, straight, and clear in what one says.
- Authenticity: being oneself; “standing in your own truth.”
- Integrity: being one’s word; doing what one says.
- Love: wishing people well; non-romantic benevolence toward the listener.
- \(V_{\text{tool}}\): ordered vocal toolbox.
- Register: approximate placement of the voice; lecture contrast is nose / throat / chest.
- Timbre: how the voice feels; lecture examples are rich, smooth, warm.
- Prosody: sing-song meta-language that carries meaning in conversation.
- Pace: speed of delivery.
- Silence: deliberate absence of speech used as emphasis.
- Pitch: contour or height pattern that changes perceived meaning and arousal.
- Volume: loudness level used either to command or to focus.
- Sodcasting: careless imposition of one’s sound on others.
- \(W_6\): six warm-up exercises used before important speaking.
- Engine/toolbox metaphor: the voice is both a set of tools and an engine that must be warmed up before use.
- Current world / desired world: closing contrast between poor communication in noisy environments and conscious speaking/listening in sound-fit environments.

## Derivation Steps

1. From powerful voice to speaking problem
   1. Start with the claim that the human voice is an extremely powerful instrument.
   2. Observe that many people nevertheless experience not being listened to.
   3. Pose the governing question: what blocks powerful listening?
   4. Move to diagnosis before prescription.

2. From the seven sins to loss of listenability
   1. Enumerate habits that speakers fall into.
   2. For each habit, show how it damages the listener’s trust or willingness to attend.
   3. Accumulate the habits into an ordered repertoire rather than isolated anecdotes.
   4. Conclude that speech fails not only because of weak vocal technique, but because of corrosive relational habits.

3. From diagnosis to HAIL
   1. Ask whether there is a positive alternative to the seven sins.
   2. Introduce the mnemonic \(\mathrm{HAIL}\).
   3. Expand each letter into a constructive foundation.
   4. Test the scheme locally with the honesty-versus-love tension.
   5. Conclude that powerful speech requires all four, not honesty alone.

4. Honesty tempered by love
   1. Consider “absolute honesty” in the abstract.
   2. Produce the counterexample of unnecessary bluntness (“you look ugly this morning”).
   3. Add love as a constraint on honesty.
   4. Conclude that truthful speech must be shaped by benevolence toward the listener.

5. From content ethics to delivery mechanics
   1. State that it is not only what we say, but how we say it.
   2. Recast the voice as a toolbox.
   3. Treat register, timbre, prosody, pace, silence, pitch, and volume as adjustable variables.
   4. Demonstrate that changing these variables changes meaning, authority, and attention.

6. Register argument
   1. Contrast voice placed “in the nose,” “in the throat,” and “in the chest.”
   2. Associate lower placement with more weight.
   3. Add the rhetorical empirical claim that lower voices are preferred in politicians.
   4. Infer that perceived authority depends partly on register choice.

7. Prosody argument
   1. Define prosody as meta-language carrying meaning.
   2. Contrast speech with prosody against speech on one note.
   3. Introduce repetitive question-ending intonation on statements.
   4. Note that this blurs the intended speech act.
   5. Conclude that prosodic range is part of communicative precision.

8. Pace, silence, and pitch
   1. Speed up delivery to demonstrate excitement.
   2. Slow down delivery to demonstrate emphasis.
   3. Insert silence to show that meaning need not be filled with “ums” and “ahs.”
   4. Change pitch contour on the same sentence.
   5. Conclude that temporal and melodic control directly modulate meaning.

9. From toolbox to warm-up
   1. Shift to cases where speech really matters.
   2. Argue that one owes oneself preparation in such moments.
   3. Use the engine metaphor: engines do not run well cold.
   4. Infer that the voice should be warmed up before important speaking.

10. Six warm-ups as protocol
   1. Deep breath and sigh to open the body and breath.
   2. Lip percussion (“bop”) to activate the lips.
   3. Lip trill to continue lip activation.
   4. Exaggerated \(la\)-sequence to wake up the tongue.
   5. Rolled \(r\) to further loosen the tongue.
   6. Siren \(we \to or\) to traverse vocal range.
   7. Conclude that the instrument is now ready for use.

11. Closing world-model
   1. Diagnose the present state as poor speaking into poor listening amid noise and bad acoustics.
   2. Ask a counterfactual question about a better world.
   3. Replace careless sound creation with conscious speaking, listening, and design.
   4. Conclude with beautiful sound and understanding as the global payoff.

## Notation Choices

- Use \(S_7\) for the ordered seven-sin repertoire.
- Treat \(S_7\) as an ordered tuple, not a set, because the lecture presents a deliberate sequence.
- Use \(\mathrm{HAIL}\) in upright letters for the mnemonic; do not introduce separate symbols for honesty/authenticity/integrity/love unless needed locally.
- Use \(V_{\text{tool}}\) for the ordered vocal-toolbox tuple.
- Keep toolbox entries in prose rather than introducing unnecessary one-letter acoustic variables.
- Use \(W_6\) for the six-step warm-up protocol.
- Use \(\Rightarrow\) only for qualitative implication or perceived association, not for a theorem or measured law.
- Use \(\not\Rightarrow\) for local negation of a naive principle, especially “absolute honesty” as sufficient.
- Use \(\neq\) for contrast of delivery contours when the same words carry different meanings.
- Keep “nose,” “throat,” and “chest” as phenomenological lecture labels, not as anatomical/acoustic precision terms.
- Keep “we \to or” for the siren exercise as a practical sound-motion notation, not a phonetic transcription.
- Avoid importing acoustic symbols such as frequency \(f\), amplitude \(A\), spectral envelopes, or decibel notation; they are not used in the lecture.
- Avoid IPA or formal intonational notation unless explicitly signposted as outside the lecture.
