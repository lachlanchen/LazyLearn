## Definitions And Objects
- \(\text{readerly presence}\): the reader feels located inside the scene and able to follow unfolding action, thought, and feeling.
- \(\text{description}\): not ornament, but the main tool for making place, mood, and situation apprehensible.
- \(\text{effective description}\): description that is vivid enough to orient and activate the reader, but lean enough not to bog the prose down.
- \(\text{urgency}\): the reader’s felt compulsion to continue; may come from psychology, phrasing, tone, or curiosity rather than overt action.
- \(\text{page-turner}\): the narrower action-driven case of urgency.
- \(\text{premise}\): the initial compressed story kernel, often one sentence.
- \(\text{scaffolding}\): early layout of major events and structural supports before full drafting.
- \(\text{notebooks}\): long pre-draft working space where scenes, questions, and visualized moments are explored.
- \(K_{\text{omniscient third}}\): the full knowledge set available to an all-knowing narrator.
- \(K_{\text{close third}}\): the restricted knowledge set tied closely to one character’s consciousness.
- \(S\): scene skeleton; the overtly observable setup.
- \(H\): hidden state; background emotional or narrative condition not yet known to the focal perceiver.
- \(D\): realized description; the actual wording, tempo, and tonal surface of the scene.
- \(\text{manifesto energy}\): high-pressure, declarative, action-leaning rhetorical force.
- \(\text{opening of doors}\): Towles’s test for a promising premise; the sense that an idea branches rapidly into many scenes, layers, and possible directions.

## Derivation Steps
1. Description to presence
   1. The lecture sets the goal as making the reader feel they are living the experience.
   2. That goal requires the reader to know where they are and how the scene is arranged.
   3. Description supplies that orientation.
   4. If the orientation is vivid but economical, the reader can then move into character and event.
   5. Therefore description functions as the gateway to presence, not as decorative excess.

2. Balance of description
   1. If detail is too sparse, the reader cannot locate the scene.
   2. If detail is too dense, the prose becomes cumbersome.
   3. The writer therefore adds and removes details while testing readerly clarity.
   4. The target is the small set of details that make the space come alive.

3. Planning to poetic surprise
   1. Towles solves setting, event, and character logistics before drafting.
   2. This removes live problem-solving pressure from the drafting moment.
   3. Reduced analytic load leaves more room for instinctive phrasing and unexpected detail.
   4. Those surprises are then sharpened in later redrafting.

4. Point of view to symbolism
   1. The lecture distinguishes omniscient third from close third.
   2. In close third, the narration is filtered through what the focal character notices and values.
   3. Meaningful details therefore arise from that consciousness rather than from outside authorial insertion.
   4. Symbolic or resonant effects emerge as consequences of inhabited voice.

5. Anti-cliche scene construction
   1. Start with a nominal scene in a known historical setting.
   2. Remove stock period markers that merely announce the decade.
   3. Ask what this specific perceiver would actually notice.
   4. Replace generic markers with focal sensory details.
   5. Result: the scene becomes concrete without becoming clichéd.

6. Hidden state to altered surface
   1. Hold the visible kitchen scene fixed.
   2. Change the mother’s unseen backstory from betrayal suffered to betrayal committed.
   3. The child still sees only behavior, gesture, and atmosphere.
   4. Tone, tempo, and diction must therefore shift even though the visible setup is similar.
   5. Later revelation should make the earlier scene retrospectively legible.

7. Draft for self to revision for reader
   1. First draft is allowed to expand through whim, fascination, digression, and excess.
   2. Revision flips the viewpoint from writer to reader.
   3. Redundancy, cliché, boredom, and private indulgence are then removed.
   4. The remaining material is what serves the story’s economy.

8. Practice to teachable craft
   1. Craft is broken into elements such as plot, setting, dialogue, tone, and perspective.
   2. Repeated writing exercises force actual use of those elements.
   3. Perspective-shifting prevents any one formula from hardening into “the” way to write a scene.
   4. Reading analytically reinforces the same craft judgments from the other side.

## Notation Choices
- Use roman text inside math mode for conceptual entities: \(\text{description}\), \(\text{presence}\), \(\text{urgency}\), \(\text{action}\).
- Use \(\to\) for causal or procedural flow.
- Use \(\Rightarrow\) for compressed editorial implication rather than strict logical proof.
- Use \(\subset\) only for the narrator-knowledge comparison:
  - \(K_{\text{close third}} \subset K_{\text{omniscient third}}\)
- Use \(S\) for visible scene skeleton, \(H\) for hidden state, and \(D\) for realized description.
- If two hidden states are contrasted, write them as \(H_1\) and \(H_2\); if the resulting descriptions differ, write \(D_1\) and \(D_2\).
- Keep time/process quantities in plain units rather than abstract symbols:
  - \(1.5\) hours for initial key elements,
  - \(3\) days for scaffolding,
  - a couple of years for notebooks,
  - \(1.5\) years for drafting,
  - \(2\)–\(3\) revision passes.
- Do not introduce operator notation, indexed formulas, or faux-physics symbols beyond the minimal schematic bank above.
- Keep “left brain / right brain” as prose unless a compact chain is needed; do not promote it into hard scientific notation.
