# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- The lecture opens by carrying forward the previous Rawls discussion: Sandel recalls the distinction between moral desert and entitlements to legitimate expectations, then shifts the terrain from income and wealth to opportunities, hiring, and admissions. The opening move matters because affirmative action is introduced not as a standalone policy dispute, but as a new test case for the deeper question whether distributive justice tracks desert.
- He first narrates the Cheryl Hopwood case in concrete terms: her educational path, her grades and test performance, the Texas admissions policy, and the law school's diversity rationale. The immediate pivot is from legal history to moral judgment: we are asked to put aside the law and ask whether Hopwood has a legitimate complaint as a matter of justice.
- The next phase is dialectical and classroom-driven. Bree raises the arbitrariness objection, Anisha replies with the argument from unequal preparation and the possibility that grades and scores mismeasure real academic potential, and Sandel sharpens that reply by asking whether race could still matter even when educational disadvantage is held fixed.
- From there the discussion broadens into rival justifications for affirmative action. David offers the compensatory or reparative rationale; Kate objects that present policy should fix causes, not outcomes; Hannah introduces the comparison with nepotism and legacy admissions; Ted insists that race-based decisions are inherently unfair; Da answers by pointing to the arbitrariness of family background as well.
- Sandel then deliberately stops the back-and-forth and reorganizes the terrain. He names three arguments that have emerged: corrective, compensatory, and diversity. This is the lecture's first major classificatory moment, and it should feel like a board-summary even though no validated board frame survives.
- Once the three arguments are on the table, he treats them in order of increasing philosophical reach. The compensatory argument is challenged by the question whether it is fair to ask Hopwood to bear costs for injustices in which she was not personally implicated; Sandel identifies the issue of group rights and collective responsibility, then explicitly sets it aside.
- He turns next to the diversity argument, first in its educational form and then in its wider civic form. This leads into the Bakke case and Harvard's brief, where Sandel presents diversity as a broad admissions criterion alongside geography, talents, and social background. The key transition is from diversity as a slogan to diversity as a claim about institutional purpose.
- That institutional-purpose argument produces the lecture's deepest Rawlsian puzzle. Sandel asks whether common-good reasoning may deny an applicant admission for the sake of a social mission, and whether Hopwood has a right to be judged only by her own achievements. The lecture here returns to the prior Rawls theme: no one morally deserves admission under a mission-neutral standard, because standards themselves depend on institutional purpose.
- Near 00:25:52 there is a visible seam: a brief flute teaser appears, then the lecture resets at 00:26:20 with “When we ended last time.” This reset should be preserved rather than hidden. It functions as a recap that re-enters the three arguments and then pushes the diversity issue to a sharper question about whether institutions may define their mission however they please.
- The final major pivot is from Rawls to Aristotle. Da challenges the claim that a private institution may define its purpose arbitrarily; Sandel tests this by comparing present-day diversity defenses with Texas segregation in the 1950s and Harvard's anti-Jewish quotas in the 1930s. When Hannah replies in terms of inclusion rather than exclusion, Sandel presses further until the debate becomes a general question: are admissions and social positions about honor, fitness, and desert, or about rights and institutionally defined entitlements?
- From there he explicitly introduces Aristotle as the thinker who refuses the Rawlsian detachment of justice from desert. The flute example, then the tennis-court example, supply the lecture's second classificatory spine: justice depends on the thing distributed, the relevant excellence, and the telos of the practice. The lecture closes by reframing the earlier affirmative-action dispute as a disagreement about the purpose of a university education and by setting up Aristotle as the next sustained topic.

## Section Outline
1. **From Rawls to Hopwood.** Open with the recap of moral desert versus entitlement, then move quickly to the Hopwood case and the Texas admissions policy so that the chapter begins where the lecture begins: a live policy case framed by a prior philosophical distinction.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Does Cheryl Hopwood have a legitimate complaint if applicants with lower academic indices were admitted under a race-conscious policy?`

2. **Arbitrariness, Educational Disadvantage, and Measured Potential.** Track Bree's claim that race is an arbitrary factor, then Anisha's reply that scores may mismeasure true academic promise under unequal schooling.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Can affirmative action be justified simply as a correction for the distorted meaning of grades and test scores?`

3. **Compensation, Diversity, and Legacy.** Follow the classroom movement from reparations and past injustice to nepotism, legacy admissions, and the broader educational value of diversity, keeping each student intervention tied to the conceptual pressure it introduces.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Is affirmative action compensation for past injustice, or is that unfair to present applicants who did not commit the wrong?`

4. **Three Arguments for Affirmative Action.** Preserve Sandel's mid-lecture reorganization into the corrective, compensatory, and diversity arguments, including his distinction between diversity for educational experience and diversity for the wider civic mission of institutions.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `What are the three distinct justifications for counting race and ethnicity in admissions, and how do they differ?`

5. **The Diversity Argument and the Rawlsian Rights Objection.** Present Harvard's Bakke brief and the University of Texas mission argument, then pivot to the objection that common-good reasoning may not override individual rights.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Does an applicant have a right to be judged only by achievements and factors within her control?`

6. **Reset Seam, Institutional Mission, and Historical Counterexamples.** Preserve the visible reset around 00:26:20, then follow Da's objection and Sandel's historical comparisons with 1950s Texas and 1930s Harvard to test whether institutional purpose can justify exclusion as well as inclusion.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Is there a principled difference between today's diversity rationale and earlier exclusionary appeals to institutional mission?`

7. **Aristotle, Flutes, and Teleological Justice.** Let the lecture's final movement become a genuine concluding section: justice as giving people their due according to the telos of the thing distributed, illustrated by flutes and then by tennis courts, with affirmative action retrospectively recast as a dispute about the purpose of the university.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Why should the best flutes go to the best flute players, and why is that answer not utilitarian?`
