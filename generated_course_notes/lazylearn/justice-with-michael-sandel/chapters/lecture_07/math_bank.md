## Definitions And Objects
- `duty`: acting from the moral law rather than from inclination, gain, appetite, or circumstance.
- `autonomy`: self-legislation; acting under a law one gives oneself.
- `dignity`: the standing attached to being the author of the law, not merely its subject.
- `pure reason`: the non-subjective source of willing that is shared across rational agents.
- `categorical imperative`: the single universal moral law produced by pure reason; lecture treats it as the formal core of morality.
- `sensible world`: the standpoint under which action is described by natural law, causation, and empirical determination.
- `intelligible world`: the standpoint under which the agent is free and capable of autonomy.
- `heteronomy`: willing governed by desire for some external object or end.
- `lie`: outright falsehood; treated as incompatible with the categorical imperative.
- `misleading truth`: a statement that is strictly true but crafted so that another person will likely be deceived.
- `white lie`: a consequence-justified falsehood, used in ordinary life to avoid hurt or discomfort.
- `actual contract`: a real agreement among concrete persons under real inequalities of power and knowledge.
- `hypothetical contract`: an “idea of reason,” not an event in history.
- `veil of ignorance`: Rawls’s device for bracketing particular facts about ourselves.
- `original position`: the equal standpoint generated by the veil of ignorance.
- `autonomy` and `reciprocity`: the two distinguishable ideals Sandel says underwrite the moral force of actual contracts.

## Derivation Steps
Duty and autonomy
1. Duty seems opposed to freedom if the law is understood as externally imposed.
2. Kant’s answer is to treat the moral law as self-imposed.
3. If the law is self-imposed, acting from duty is acting under a law one has willed.
4. Therefore duty and autonomy coincide rather than conflict.
5. Dignity follows because the agent is author as well as subject of the law.

Universality of the moral law
1. Self-legislation seems to threaten many private moral laws.
2. Sandel shifts the subject of choice from the private self to pure reason.
3. Pure reason is not contingent on personal circumstance.
4. Therefore the law willed by reason is universal rather than merely individual.
5. This yields one supreme moral law rather than many consciences in conflict.

Possibility of morality
1. If the human being is only empirical, every choice is driven by desire for some object.
2. Choice driven by desire is heteronomous.
3. Heteronomy does not amount to freedom.
4. Kant therefore distinguishes the sensible and intelligible standpoints.
5. From the intelligible standpoint, the will can regard itself as autonomous.
6. Because we inhabit both standpoints, there is a standing gap between what is and what ought to be.
7. Therefore morality cannot be empirical science.

Murderer-at-the-door structure
1. Kant forbids lying categorically.
2. Constant objects that consequences make this implausible in the murderer case.
3. Sandel refuses a consequence-based exception because that would surrender Kant’s framework.
4. The lecture then searches for a truthful evasion rather than a falsehood.
5. This search produces the distinction between lie and misleading truth.

Lie versus misleading truth
1. A lie says what is false in order to deceive.
2. A misleading truth says what is true while hoping the hearer will still be misled.
3. On a consequentialist reading, both may look equivalent because both aim at similar outcomes.
4. Sandel’s Kantian defense relocates the distinction to formal fidelity to the moral law.
5. The misleading truth still preserves truth at the level of utterance.
6. Sandel therefore treats it as paying “homage” to duty, unlike the outright lie.

Hypothetical versus actual contract
1. Actual constitutional agreement reflects interests, bargaining power, and unequal knowledge.
2. Therefore actual agreement does not guarantee justice.
3. Kant’s political contract must then be hypothetical rather than historical.
4. Rawls gives this structure operational form through the veil of ignorance.
5. Equality in the original position is meant to neutralize the distortions present in actual bargaining.

Two sources of contractual obligation
1. First source: consent binds because the obligation is self-imposed.
2. Second source: benefit binds because reciprocity can generate obligation.
3. Lobster case with performance and benefit isolates reciprocity.
4. Immediate cancellation case tests whether consent alone still carries force.
5. Later cases show that actual contracts often mix both sources.
6. Final result: the moral force of actual contracts is dual, not unitary.

Limits of actual contracts
1. Even if two parties agree, the terms may still be unfair.
2. Unequal bargaining power limits autonomy in practice.
3. Unequal knowledge limits reciprocity in practice.
4. Therefore actual contracts are morally forceful but not self-justifying.
5. Rawls’s hypothetical contract is introduced to model the ideals without those contingencies.
