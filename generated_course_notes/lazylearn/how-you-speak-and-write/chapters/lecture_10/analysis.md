# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
The lecture opens by manufacturing failure: a deliberately bad wilderness passage names fear and comfort but gives us no evidence, then immediately asks, “What’s wrong with this picture?” That opening creates the first problem-state for the chapter: the reader is being told what to feel instead of being allowed to infer it.

From there, the lecturer makes an important corrective pivot: show-don’t-tell is not an absolute ban on telling. The Secret Garden example and the “showing dramatizes, telling summarizes” distinction let us move from polemic to balance, and Andrew Stanton’s “2+2, not 4” metaphor reframes showing as controlled inference rather than decoration.

The lecture then briefly detours into literary history, using realism, Percy Lubbock, and Janice Hardy to explain why the advice arose and what it is trying to prevent: authorial intrusion that breaks immersion. That historical aside is not the main payload, but it motivates the six-principle program that follows.

The main body of the chapter should preserve the six-principle march in order: evidence, concreteness, sensory specificity, restraint about body language, dialogue, and narrative voice. Each principle is introduced by a claim, sharpened by a before/after example, and then generalized into a practical drafting or revision test.

Late in the lecture, the argument widens again from sentence-level craft to pacing, genre, and narrative voice. The closing movement matters: the lecturer does not end with “always show,” but with “show, don’t just tell,” then loops back to the opening wilderness passage with the Delia Owens example, turning the opening failure into a resolved model.
