# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- Sandel begins by recapping the unresolved problem from Mill rather than starting fresh. The opening move is a deliberate return to the Shakespeare-versus-Simpsons experiment so that the lecture can pivot from Mill’s attempted repair of utilitarianism to the question of whether rights and justice can really be defended on utilitarian grounds.
- The first transition is from Mill’s defense of higher pleasures to Mill’s defense of rights. Sandel then tightens the pressure by distinguishing two worries: not only might long-run utility fail to justify rights in hard cases, but even if it does justify them, that may still be the wrong reason to respect persons.
- The organ-transplant example is the first major stress test. Sandel uses it to force the issue from “what produces good results?” to “what kind of reason counts as a morally adequate reason not to use a person?”
- He then motivates the turn away from utilitarianism by stating what is needed next: a stronger account of the good life and a stronger account of rights. This is the lecture’s main pivot, and it introduces libertarianism not as an isolated doctrine but as one candidate answer to Mill’s failure.
- Once libertarianism is introduced, Sandel immediately operationalizes it by asking what the state may legitimately do. The lecture’s structure becomes increasingly crisp here: he gives the right to liberty, quotes Nozick, and then enumerates three kinds of legislation or policy the libertarian rejects.
- From abstract principle he moves to policy and numbers. The Bill Gates and Michael Jordan examples give the lecture its most concrete quantitative spine, because Sandel wants us to feel what redistribution looks like when attached to actual wealth, hourly earnings, and tax shares.
- The next transition is from lecture exposition to classroom dialectic. Sandel uses student objections to widen the field from utilitarian redistribution to questions of entitlement, need, desert, equality of opportunity, democratic consent, and debt to society.
- Nozick’s harder claim marks the lecture’s sharpest logical escalation: taxation of earnings is presented as taking the fruits of labor, then as claiming labor itself, then as forced labor, and finally as a denial of self-possession. This chain gives the lecture its deepest quasi-formal structure.
- A later recap beat returns to libertarianism from a slightly different angle, via Milton Friedman, Social Security, and the Salem Fire Corporation. This reset should be preserved in the notes as a functional reprise rather than treated as repetition to be cut away.
- The lecture ends by pushing beneath taxation and redistribution to the underlying premise of self-ownership. Victoria’s objection then becomes the final philosophical hinge: if self-possession is doubtful, how much of the libertarian edifice survives? Sandel closes by pointing us to Locke, preserving an intentionally unfinished ending.

## Section Outline
1. **From Mill’s Repair to the Need for Stronger Rights.** Open by recapping Mill’s attempt to distinguish higher pleasures and then move to his chapter-five defense of justice and rights. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Even if respecting rights maximizes welfare in the long run, is that the right reason to respect persons?`

2. **Libertarianism as a Strong Theory of Rights.** Introduce libertarianism exactly as Sandel does: as a theory that treats persons as separate beings with separate lives, not as containers of utility. Bring in Nozick’s formulation that rights are so strong and far-reaching that they raise the question of what, if anything, the state may do.

3. **The Minimal State and Its Three Prohibitions.** Preserve the lecture’s numbered progression through paternalist legislation, morals legislation, and redistribution. This section should sound like Sandel unfolding the consequences of one basic principle rather than listing libertarian slogans.

4. **Redistribution, Historical Justice, and the Entitlement View.** Move from the general critique of redistribution to the claim that a distribution cannot be judged by its pattern alone; we must ask how it arose. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Can we judge a distribution by the end-state alone, or only by the history of acquisition and transfer?`

5. **Bill Gates, Michael Jordan, and the Classroom Objections.** Use the Gates and Jordan examples to make redistribution concrete and then track the student objections in the order they arise: social contribution, need versus desert, equality of opportunity, and debt to society. Keep the classroom’s back-and-forth alive, because the lecture’s rhythm here depends on live resistance to the libertarian claim.

6. **From Taxation to Forced Labor to Self-Possession.** Present Nozick’s escalated chain that taxation is not only theft but morally akin to forced labor and, in the limit, slavery. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Is taxation for redistribution really morally equivalent to forced labor?`

7. **Democracy, the Minimal State Revisited, and the Turn to Locke.** Preserve the later reset through Friedman on Social Security and the private fire-company example, then return to the classroom exchange about democratic consent and constitutional limits. End where Sandel ends: with Victoria’s challenge to self-possession and the transition to Locke as the next test of the libertarian premise.

- [transcript-backed] Locke’s closing chain, as narrated by Sandel:
  \[
  \text{self-possession} \to \text{ownership of labor} \to \text{mixing labor with unowned things} \to \text{private property}.
  \]
  This is only a teaser at the end of the lecture, but it should appear in the chapter plan because it explains the ending.
