# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- The lecture opens by explicitly turning from Kant and Rawls to Aristotle. Sandel first recalls the modern attempt to detach justice from desert and virtue, then immediately states Aristotle’s reversal: justice is giving people what they deserve, and reasoning about justice must therefore reason about the telos of the practice in question.
- He does not linger on abstraction for long. The opening flute example serves as the first compact model: equal things go to equal persons only after we ask “equal in what respect?”, and Aristotle’s answer is that the relevant respect is fixed by the purpose of the thing being distributed.
- From that point Sandel widens the frame. The pivot is important: he says that teleological reasoning is not easy to dispense with in ethics and politics, and he announces two applications that will test the claim, first politics, then golf.
- The politics discussion comes first and is the lecture’s main constructive movement. Sandel contrasts modern distributive justice, usually framed around income, wealth, and opportunity, with Aristotle’s focus on offices, honors, citizenship, and rule; then he asks what politics is for and answers that it is for forming character, cultivating virtue, and realizing the good life.
- He does not leave that answer hanging. The next pivot is motivational: why should political participation matter to human flourishing at all? Sandel gives a two-stage answer, first from the Politics by way of human beings as naturally political and linguistic, then from the Nicomachean Ethics by way of happiness as activity in accordance with virtue and virtue as something acquired through practice rather than rule-following.
- That second answer lets him return from ethics to institutions. The lecture closes the politics segment by tying civic virtue, deliberation among equals, and the honoring of exemplary citizens such as Pericles back to the distribution of political authority; this is the moment where teleological and honorific justice are shown to work together.
- Only then does he pivot to the contemporary controversy over Casey Martin and the golf cart. This is not a digression: the golf case is introduced precisely to show the link between rights-claims and arguments about the telos of a practice, and to show that debates about purpose are also debates about what excellences should be honored.
- The first pass through the golf case ends with the Supreme Court dispute and Scalia’s anti-Aristotelian dissent, which casts games as mere amusement. That stopping point should be preserved because the episode later resumes across a clear seam with “When we ended last time,” recapping the golf-cart issue before deepening it.
- After the recap seam, Sandel uses the classroom exchange around Jenny’s proposal to sharpen the case. The question is no longer just whether Casey Martin should get a cart, but whether making carts available to everyone solves the fairness problem or instead changes the very nature of the game.
- From that exchange Sandel extracts two explicit morals. First, the right-claim depends on whether walking is essential to golf; second, the dispute is also about honor, because professional golfers have a stake in whether golf counts as an athletic competition rather than merely a game of skill.
- The lecture’s final movement is evaluative rather than expository. Sandel raises the freedom objection to teleology, introduces Aristotle’s defense of slavery as the most troubling application, and then returns to the higher-level confrontation with Kant and Rawls: is justice a matter of fit between persons and roles, or must freedom require that the right be prior to the good?
- The ending is therefore not a conclusion but a setup. The lecture closes by framing two large questions for the next discussion: whether the right is prior to the good, and whether freedom means choosing one’s ends or discovering one’s nature.

## Section Outline
1. **From Rawls and Kant to Aristotle.** Open with the modern anti-desert view, then state Aristotle’s counterclaim that justice concerns what people deserve and requires reasoning about telos.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Equal things to equal persons, but equal in what respect?`

2. **Flutes, Telos, and the Honorific Turn.** Use the flute example to establish the lecture’s first formal spine: the purpose of the thing distributed fixes the relevant excellence, and disputes about telos are already disputes about what qualities deserve honor.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Why should the best flutes go to the best flute players?`

3. **Politics as the Distribution of Offices and Honors.** Follow Sandel’s shift from modern distributions of wealth and opportunity to Aristotle’s concern with citizenship, authority, and political rule, then derive the criterion of rule from the telos of politics as the good life.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `If politics is for the good life, who should rule?`

4. **Why Political Life Matters to Human Flourishing.** Preserve the two-step answer: first, human beings realize their nature only in a polis through language and deliberation; second, virtue and happiness require practice, habituation, and the discerning of particulars rather than book learning alone.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Why can’t a person live a fully good life outside politics?`

5. **Casey Martin, Golf, and the Telos of a Game.** Introduce the golf-cart case exactly as Sandel does, as a rights controversy that forces us to ask what golf is for and whether walking is essential to the game.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Does Casey Martin have a right to use a golf cart, or does that depend on the purpose of golf?`

6. **Recap Seam, Fairness, and the Honor of Sport.** Preserve the mid-episode reset and the Jenny/Da/Michael exchange, because this is where the lecture most clearly turns the golf case into a dispute not only about fairness but about whether golf is truly an athletic competition and what excellences it honors.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Would allowing everyone to use a cart solve the fairness problem, or would it change the game?`

7. **Freedom, Slavery, and the Objection to Teleology.** End with Sandel’s transition from golf to the larger challenge: whether justice as fit leaves room for freedom, why slavery is the hardest case for Aristotle, and how the dispute with Kant and Rawls returns in the form of the right-versus-good question.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `If justice depends on telos and fit, what room is left for freedom and individual choice?`
