# Narrative Map
## Opening Setup
We begin with a deliberately strong first impression: Locke looks like a natural ally of the libertarian because he affirms pre-political rights to life, liberty, and property and treats property as natural rather than legally manufactured. Sandel uses that opening not to settle the matter, but to place a live expectation in front of us, so that the rest of the lecture can test, qualify, and finally partially undo it.

The lecture then unfolds in two large movements. First, we work through the state of nature, unalienable rights, and Locke’s account of property before government; second, after a recap-style reset in the broadcast edit, we turn to consent, legitimate government, taxation, implied consent, and conscription. The rhythm depends on this sequence: first build Locke into a plausible ally, then expose where the alliance weakens.

## Beat Sequence
1. **Locke introduced as the likely libertarian ally.** Sandel is trying to establish the strongest prima facie case for Locke: natural rights are prior to law, and no government, however democratic, may override them. This appears first because the whole lecture is organized around testing that initial appearance, and it leads directly into the need to define what a “natural” right could mean before politics exists.

2. **The state of nature is liberty, but not license.** Sandel introduces the state of nature as the conceptual setting in which natural rights can be understood, then immediately blocks the easy inference that freedom means unrestricted choice. This comes here because the opening claim about pre-political rights would otherwise remain empty, and it leads into the sharper question of what constrains us if no legislature is yet present. `[Q&A candidate: If we are free and equal in the state of nature, why are we not free to do whatever we want?]`

3. **The source of constraint: first God, then reason, then unalienability.** Sandel gives two foundations for the law of nature, first theological and then rational, and uses them to explain why rights cannot be given away or violated, even by oneself. This beat appears now because he must explain the “not license” claim before turning to property, and it leads into the paradox of unalienable rights as somehow more deeply ours because they are not disposable.

4. **From self-possession to labor to property.** Sandel now turns to Locke’s central constructive move: property in the person grounds property in labor, and labor mixed with the unowned yields private property, subject to the “enough and as good” proviso. This arrives only after unalienability has been clarified, because otherwise the property argument would look like a brute assertion, and it leads into the question whether this account really works in practice. `[Q&A candidate: How can private property arise before government or consent?]`

5. **Testing the property argument through modern and historical disputes.** Sandel first uses the drug-patent case to keep alive the intuition that labor or creation can generate ownership, then turns to the class exchange about Native Americans, colonization, enclosure, and common use to press the argument morally and historically. This beat appears here because the lecture has just finished constructing Locke’s theory and now needs to see whether it persuades or merely rationalizes, and it leads to the broader question of what natural property rights can mean once society and government arrive.

6. **Pivot from property to consent.** After the mid-lecture recap reset, Sandel explicitly names consent as Locke’s “second big idea” and asks what work it does, returning to the state of nature to ask why anyone would leave it. This comes at this point because the lecture has finished the property half and now needs the mechanism by which people enter civil society, and it leads into the “inconveniences” of private enforcement as the motive for political agreement.

7. **Consent creates civil society, but natural rights remain.** Sandel explains that we leave the state of nature because everyone is an executor of the law of nature, punishment overshoots, and insecurity spreads; consent therefore transfers enforcement power to a political community governed by majority rule. This appears now because the need for government must be established before its limits can be assessed, and it leads directly into the central puzzle: if natural rights survive entry into society, what exactly may the majority decide? `[Q&A candidate: If natural rights remain in force, what exactly does consent authorize the majority to do?]`

8. **Taxation, implied consent, and the emergence of the major puzzle.** Sandel uses Locke’s own text on taxation to sharpen the tension between “no taking without consent” and “consent of the majority,” then extends the problem through student questions about birth into society, tacit or implied consent, and tax obligation. This belongs here because the abstract puzzle about consent needs concrete pressure, and it leads into the still harder case of life itself in conscription. `[Q&A candidate: Can those who never explicitly consented still be bound by government and taxation?]`

9. **Conscription, arbitrary power, and the final verdict on Locke.** Sandel raises the stakes from property to life, then reconstructs Locke’s likely answer: the core prohibition is not every burden imposed by government, but arbitrary singling out, whereas general law enacted through proper procedure may still bind all. This closes the lecture because it yields the final mixed verdict: Locke limits government against arbitrariness, yet leaves more room for state power than libertarians would like, and so the lecture can end by handing the unresolved issue of consent forward to markets. `[Q&A candidate: If rights are unalienable, how can anyone be bound by taxation or conscription under majority rule?]`

## Transition Cues
- Sandel opens with a clean declarative pivot: “Today we turn to John Locke.”
- He frequently advances by first granting the strongest version of a position: Locke “on the face of it” looks like a powerful ally.
- He sharpens the lecture by pairing a claim with an immediate correction: liberty, yes; license, no.
- He likes to pivot by asking what must now be explained: “where does this constraint come from?” or “how can there be a right to private property even before government?”
- Once the constructive argument is in place, he tests it by asking whether it is “successful” or “persuasive,” then hands the floor to objections.
- In the second half he uses recap language to reset the audience: “Last time…” and then “Today I want to turn to the question of consent.”
- He repeatedly converts exposition into puzzle form: “what is the work of consent?” and “what can the majority decide?”
- He often marks a major turn by briefly seeming to concede the other side, then retracting it: Locke may gladden the libertarian’s heart, but “those hearts should not be so quickly gladdened.”
- He ends by widening the aperture rather than resolving everything, turning from government to “the limits of consent” in markets.

## Recurring Motifs
- **Ally or critic?** The lecture repeatedly frames Locke as first an ally of libertarianism, then a weaker and more compromised one.
- **Natural before conventional.** Sandel keeps returning to the distinction between what exists prior to government and what government later defines.
- **Liberty versus license.** This is the first major distinction and quietly structures the whole lecture.
- **Unalienability as paradox.** Rights are said to be ours in a deeper sense precisely because they are not fully disposable.
- **From abstract principle to case pressure.** Sandel regularly moves from a conceptual chain to a live example: airline tickets, patents, colonization, taxation, implied consent, conscription.
- **Consent under strain.** Consent is introduced as obvious and familiar, then progressively shown to be philosophically unstable.
- **Arbitrary versus general law.** Near the end, this becomes the key distinction that allows Sandel to reconstruct Locke’s way out.
- **America in the background.** The lecture keeps circling back to the historical question whether Locke’s property theory helped justify colonial enclosure.

## Pacing Risks
- A draft writer may compress the opening too quickly into “Locke believes in natural rights” and lose the deliberate suspense created by presenting him first as a libertarian ally.
- The liberty/license distinction can disappear if the state of nature is summarized too briskly; that would flatten the lecture’s first real conceptual obstacle.
- The two foundations of constraint, God and reason, should not be collapsed into a single generic source of morality; Sandel stages them in sequence for a reason.
- The airline-ticket example may seem lightweight, but cutting it would remove the lecture’s clearest pedagogical handle on unalienability.
- The labor theory of property should not be reduced to a slogan about “mixing labor”; the lecture carefully builds person, labor, appropriation, proviso, and land ownership as linked steps.
- The patent dispute is easy to treat as a side example, but in the lecture it serves as a modern bridge before the historical objection about colonization.
- The Native American discussion must not be summarized as mere classroom chatter. It is where Sandel tests whether Locke offers a justification or a rationalization.
- There is a real broadcast splice around the short tax exchange before the “Last time…” recap. If a draft treats the transcript as perfectly continuous there, the chapter’s rhythm will feel wrong.
- The consent half is vulnerable to over-compression because it contains many student exchanges. Those exchanges are doing philosophical work by generating the puzzles about tacit consent, tax obligation, and withdrawal.
- The ending should not sound conclusive. Sandel closes by leaving consent under pressure and handing the unresolved problem toward the next lecture on markets.
