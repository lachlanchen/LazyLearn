## Definitions And Objects
- `NaNoWriMo challenge`: a one-month writing challenge with a nominal target of 50,000 words.
- `internal editor`: the perfectionist or self-censoring mode the challenge is meant to suppress.
- `borrowed structure`: extracting the fundamental pattern of a story or subgenre and rebuilding it with new material.
- `hallmark scenes`: the repeatable structural beats Sanderson identifies inside a subgenre.
- `training wheels`: Sanderson’s metaphor for structural borrowing as temporary support rather than creative bondage.
- `monologue`: a first-person self-explanation exercise used to discover a character’s voice and history.
- `epigraph`: a short text fragment, often placed at the start of a chapter, which the monologue exercise may generate.
- `epistolary novel`: a novel written through journals, letters, or similar in-world documents.
- `want` \(W\): what the character consciously pursues.
- `need` \(N\): what the character actually requires for deeper fulfillment or change.
- `obstacles` \(O\): the conditions preventing the character from obtaining what they want or need.
- `viewpoint character`: the character through whom the story is most personally and dynamically experienced.
- `protagonist`: the character who is changing most, bearing the most conflict, or striving most actively.
- `promise`: the kind of story motion or satisfaction the narrative offers the reader.
- `progress`: the felt movement toward that promise; the lecture treats this as the bulk of the story.
- `payoff`: the later fulfillment of the story’s promise.
- `travel progress`: advancement measured by movement through space toward a destination.
- `information progress`: advancement measured by accumulation and clarification of clues.
- `false clue`: a backward or sideways step in a mystery’s information channel.
- `signposting`: making the current kind of progress legible to the reader.
- `progress channel`: the dominant axis along which the reader experiences forward motion.
- `priming the mind`: using low-attention time to rehearse the next scene before drafting.

## Derivation Steps
NaNoWriMo working rate
1. Take the spoken target of 50,000 words.
2. Treat the challenge as one month, approximated by 30 days.
3. Divide \(50{,}000\) by \(30\).
4. Get about \(1{,}667\) words per day.
5. Use Sanderson’s practical rounding of about \(1{,}700\) words per day.

Borrowed structure
1. Start from a story or subgenre we admire.
2. Ask what makes that kind of story work.
3. Extract the hallmark scenes or functional beats.
4. Strip those beats down to a fundamental structure.
5. Rebuild that structure with new characters, a new problem, and a new arc.
6. Optionally transpose the structure into a different genre.

Monologue to character discovery
1. Assume the character is speaking directly to us.
2. Ask how they would explain an important part of their life.
3. Write that explanation in a concentrated burst.
4. Use it to discover voice, history, and attitude.
5. Mine useful fragments for epigraphs or related forms.
6. Decide later whether any of it belongs in the final book.

Want/need to plot generation
1. Ask what the character wants.
2. Ask what the character needs.
3. Ask how those two differ.
4. Ask why the character can have neither easily.
5. Convert those answers into obstacles.
6. Let those obstacles generate plot pressure.
7. Keep the story centered on the character’s stakes.

Choosing the viewpoint character
1. Check whether the current character is only observing events.
2. Identify who is actually changing most.
3. Identify who is under the greatest conflict.
4. Identify who is striving hardest for what they want.
5. Prefer that character as protagonist or viewpoint center.

Promise, progress, payoff
1. Identify the promise the story is making to the reader.
2. Choose the kind of progress that will realize that promise.
3. Break that progress into visible intermediate steps.
4. Allow reversals, diversions, or false leads without losing the channel.
5. Deliver the payoff that matches the original promise.

Travel progress
1. Define a starting point and an end point.
2. Let movement through space measure narrative advance.
3. Show detours or setbacks as temporary reversals.
4. Keep the destination visible enough that progress remains legible.

Information progress
1. Begin with an unresolved question or mystery.
2. Add clues that accumulate over time.
3. Allow some clues to mislead or send the reader backward.
4. Use the growing body of information to sharpen the picture.
5. Make the reader feel increasing clarity even before the final answer.

Pacing diagnosis
1. Notice the reader’s complaint that the story feels slow.
2. Ask what kind of progress the story promised.
3. Ask whether that progress is being clearly signposted.
4. Check whether the signposted genre expectation matches the actual progress channel.
5. If not, the story will feel stalled even when events occur.

Priming the mind
1. Use commute, exercise, chores, or similar low-attention time.
2. Ask what the next scene should do.
3. Ask what makes that scene interesting or distinctive.
4. Mentally rehearse the next block of writing.
5. Sit down already knowing the immediate direction of the next pages.
6. Draft with reduced startup friction.

## Notation Choices
- Keep notation minimal; this lecture is procedural, not symbolic.
- Use \(W\) for what the character wants.
- Use \(N\) for what the character needs.
- Use \(O\) for obstacles blocking \(W\) and \(N\).
- Use the prose triad `promise -> progress -> payoff` rather than abstract symbols unless compression is necessary.
- Use `\to` for structural or narrative progression.
- Use `\Rightarrow` for causal consequence, especially reader-response claims.
- Use `\neq` only where the lecture insists on a meaningful distinction, especially \(W \neq N\).
- Default prose terms: `viewpoint character`, `protagonist`, `progress channel`, `signposting`.
- Avoid introducing extra variables for geography or clue counts unless a later draft genuinely needs them.
- Treat all displayed formulas as editorial summaries unless the transcript itself states the relation almost verbatim.
