## Definitions And Objects
- `character`: a constructed person assembled from inferred motives, values, behavior, and voice.
- `traits`: outer, visible features or behaviors.
- `mannerisms`: repeated behavioral habits beneath traits.
- `core inner values`: deeper commitments such as honesty, fidelity, perseverance, equality, freedom.
- `voice`: the reader-heard effect produced by diction, register, syntax, cadence, and stance.
- `drama`: collision produced by incompatible values, incompatible agents, or conflict between human aims and the world.
- `predication`: the sentence kernel consisting of the main subject and main verb.
- `register`: social/formal level of speech; also carries class and stance signals.
- `tension`: the felt rise of stakes inside a scene or across a book.
- `hook`: early elevation of tension to pull the reader in.
- `exposition`: orientation phase after the hook; who, where, stakes, situation.
- `rising action`: middle phase where each resolution creates a larger instability.
- `climax`: point where stakes cannot rise further.
- `denouement`: consequence/release phase after climax; Powers also glosses it as the “tying” and then release of the knot.
- `reader expectation`: the running forecast of what word, image, or turn is likely to come next.

## Derivation Steps
Character onion to interior drama
1. Start from visible `traits`.
2. Infer deeper `mannerisms` behind those traits.
3. Infer `core inner values` beneath the mannerisms.
4. Put two values into a situation where both cannot be preserved.
5. Force a choice.
6. The forced loss produces interior drama.

Three levels of drama
1. Begin with a person forced to choose between incompatible values.
2. This yields `person vs self`.
3. Add another person with a conflicting value hierarchy.
4. This yields `person vs person`.
5. Add a world whose conditions may resist both agents’ plans.
6. This yields `person vs world`.

Voice to character to drama
1. Choose words.
2. Those words fix `register`.
3. Arrange them syntactically.
4. Register plus syntax plus cadence generate `voice`.
5. Voice lets the reader hear how a person explains and performs themselves.
6. From that heard behavior, the reader infers `character`.
7. Once character is in collision, `drama` follows.

Sentence classes from predication
1. Find the main subject and main verb.
2. Put them early for front-loaded action or shock.
3. Delay them with modifiers for suspense or concealment.
4. Split subject and verb for rarer suspense/comic/key-change effects.
5. Use sentence placement of predication to mimic the mental state being described.

Reader expectation and surprise
1. The reader continuously predicts what comes next.
2. Sentence openings and endings are high-expectation positions.
3. Establish a likely continuation.
4. Replace the expected end word with a less likely but apt one.
5. The local surprise slightly raises tension and changes tonal color.

Form from tension
1. Start with a slightly elevated tension state.
2. Use that as the `hook`.
3. Relax enough to orient the reader: `exposition`.
4. Let each solved problem create a larger instability.
5. This generates `rising action`.
6. Continue until stakes cannot rise further.
7. That point is `climax`.
8. Release the result back into the world as `denouement`.

Fiction and value shift
1. Facts can be apprehended intellectually.
2. Intellectual apprehension alone does not guarantee changed values.
3. Story recruits affect and identification.
4. Identification temporarily shifts empathy.
5. Shifted empathy alters what the reader is moved to do.

## Notation Choices
- Use `\Rightarrow` for causal or generative claims: voice generates character, character generates drama.
- Use `\to` for ordered layers or temporal/formal progression: onion layers, structural sequence.
- Use `+` only for jointly active ingredients in a conflict setup, not as literal algebra.
- Use `S` for main subject and `V` for main verb in sentence-pattern shorthand.
- Normalize the conflict labels as `person vs self`, `person vs person`, `person vs world` in the notes, while remembering the transcript sometimes quotes the older school phrasing `man versus ...`.
- Keep `predication` as the formal term; define it once as `main subject + main verb`.
- Keep `register`, `voice`, `character`, `drama`, `form`, and `tension` in upright text inside equations: `\text{...}`.
- Treat `$P(w_{n+1}\mid w_1,\dots,w_n)$` as editorial shorthand only; it is not lecture notation.
- Present the structure graph first as a four-part model, then append `denouement` explicitly when discussing aftermath.
