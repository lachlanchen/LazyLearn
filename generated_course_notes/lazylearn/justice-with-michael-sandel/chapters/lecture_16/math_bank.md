## Definitions And Objects

- `\text{human dignity}`
  The status Kant links to persons and that the German constitution treats as inviolable.

- `\text{inviolable}`
  Not to be compromised; the lecture's strongest deontic term at the opening.

- `\text{means}`
  Using a person instrumentally to obtain some further result.

- `\text{end}`
  Respecting a person as possessing worth not reducible to usefulness.

- `\text{good purpose}`
  The saving of life or pursuit of some beneficial consequence; explicitly said not to settle permissibility in the Kantian view.

- `\text{kidnapper}`
  Magnus Gaffgen, the guilty agent in the original case.

- `\text{child}`
  Jakob von Metzler, the kidnapped victim whose endangered life drives the emergency structure.

- `\text{innocent daughter}`
  The 14-year-old daughter in the escalated hypothetical; introduced to strip away the guilty-target convenience.

- `1`
  The single person harmed in the escalated utilitarian tradeoff.

- `10`
  The larger number of innocents saved in the escalated aggregation case.

- `\text{responsibility for omission}`
  The utilitarian claim that failing to act when one could save the child makes one responsible for the death.

- `\text{good end reasoning}`
  The war reporter's phrase-pattern for how torture is actually defended in practice.

## Derivation Steps

1. Constitutional principle to hard case
   1. The lecture begins with the claim that human dignity is inviolable.
   2. Sandel then asks what happens when respecting dignity blocks an act that might save an innocent life.
   3. Therefore the chapter's first formal tension is:
      \[
      \text{inviolable dignity}
      \quad \text{vs.} \quad
      \text{life-saving emergency}
      \]

2. Emergency case to rights violation
   1. The police threaten torture to obtain the child's location.
   2. The threat succeeds in producing information.
   3. The kidnapper is convicted of murder.
   4. The police chief is also convicted for violating the kidnapper's rights.
   5. Therefore the lecture forces the audience to distinguish:
      \[
      \text{effective threat}
      \neq
      \text{permissible threat}
      \]

3. Kantian prohibition
   1. A person's dignity is not forfeited even by terrible deeds.
   2. Torture uses the person to get something out of him.
   3. Using a person as a means is not respecting him as an end.
   4. Therefore torture is barred even when aimed at a good result.

4. Utilitarian counter from the child's dignity
   1. The child is locked up and dying.
   2. Refusal to act may permit the child's death.
   3. The utilitarian asks why the kidnapper's dignity should outweigh the child's.
   4. Therefore the conflict becomes:
      \[
      \text{dignity of perpetrator}
      \quad \text{vs.} \quad
      \text{dignity and life of victim}
      \]

5. Omission-responsibility argument
   1. Suppose one can save the child.
   2. Suppose one does not act.
   3. Then, the utilitarian says, one is responsible for the death that follows.
   4. Therefore omission is treated as morally loaded, not neutral.

6. Escalation to the innocent daughter
   1. The kidnapper still will not talk.
   2. Sandel changes the target from the guilty man to his innocent daughter.
   3. In the one-for-one case, the utilitarian initially says no.
   4. Therefore the lecture isolates innocence as a separate variable from numbers.

7. Numerical aggregation
   1. Sandel raises the number of children at stake.
   2. The utilitarian concedes increasing pressure as the number rises.
   3. The case becomes:
      \[
      \text{torture }1\text{ innocent}
      \Longrightarrow
      \text{save }10\text{ innocents}
      \]
   4. The explicit rationale becomes:
      \[
      10 > 1
      \]
   5. Therefore the lecture reaches the point where morality is said to become a matter of numbers.

8. Experiential Kantian reply
   1. The war reporter recalls actual victims tortured under ``good end'' reasoning.
   2. She claims such reasoning is exactly what licenses abuse in practice.
   3. Therefore a fixed prohibition is needed to block the use of persons as means.
   4. The conclusion is:
      \[
      \text{Kantian thinking}
      \Longrightarrow
      \text{protection of human rights}
      \]

## Notation Choices

- Use `\text{...}` for all displayed expressions. The lecture's formal content is conceptual, juridical, and comparative rather than algebraic.

- Use `\Longrightarrow` for argumentative consequence, not causal mechanism or legal entailment.

- Use `\neq` for the central Kantian distinction:
  \[
  \text{use as a means} \neq \text{respect as an end}.
  \]

- Use `\stackrel{?}{\Longleftrightarrow}` or `\stackrel{?}{=}` only for contested equivalences introduced by the lecture's pressure, not for settled doctrine.

- Keep bare numerals `1` and `10` only where the transcript explicitly makes the counting structure salient.

- Treat
  \[
  10 > 1
  \]
  as editorial shorthand for utilitarian aggregation, not as a speaker-stated formula.

- Avoid introducing a utility function such as `U`, payoff matrices, or expected-value notation. The lecture never formalizes the view that way.

- Avoid introducing modal or deontic symbols such as `\Box`, `O`, or `P`. The lecture states prohibitions in ordinary language.

- If the eventual LaTeX preamble does not support `\not\Rightarrow`, replace with prose such as
  \[
  \text{good purpose does not imply permissible torture}.
  \]
