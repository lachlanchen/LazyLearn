# Narrative Map
## Opening Setup
Sandel opens by refusing to begin with doctrine. He gives us a story, tightens the situation step by step, and immediately asks for a vote before offering any vocabulary. That opening matters because the lecture wants our first contact with justice to be a pressured judgment, not a definition.

The setup also establishes the lecture’s working method: we start from what we would do, then ask why, then test whether those reasons survive a nearby variation. The chapter should therefore open in the same spirit, with direct exposition and a felt problem before any conceptual naming.

## Beat Sequence
1. The runaway trolley and the first poll. Sandel is trying to secure an initial moral intuition under controlled conditions: one can kill one to save five. It appears first because he wants a clean, numerically simple case, and it leads naturally into the demand for reasons once the class splits and votes.

2. Majority reasons and minority resistance. He next establishes that raw votes are not enough; what matters is the principle people think they are endorsing, and he immediately surfaces both a consequentialist defense and a categorical fear about genocide or totalitarian logic. This appears here to turn polling into argument, and it leads into the next move because once a principle has been stated, Sandel can test it by changing the case. [Q&A candidate: Why does saving five by turning the trolley feel different from deliberately killing one?]

3. The bridge case and the breakdown of the first principle. Sandel introduces the fat man not to add color, but to hold the arithmetic fixed while changing agency, involvement, and the character of the act. It appears at this moment to destabilize the class’s first maxim, and it leads forward because the discussion now needs a sharper account of what exactly changed.

4. Attempts to reconcile the two trolley cases. He presses the class through distinctions about active choice, direct action, prior involvement, and even the trap-door variant, showing that the intuitive contrast is harder to state cleanly than it first seemed. This beat appears here because Sandel wants us to feel the strain of explanation before he broadens the field, and it leads to the medical cases by showing that the issue is not exhausted by trolley language alone. [Q&A candidate: Why does pushing, or even trap-door turning, still seem worse than steering?]

5. Doctor triage and transplant surgeon. Sandel now varies the setting while preserving the one-versus-many structure, first with emergency-room triage and then with organ harvesting from a healthy patient. This appears here to test whether the class is really committed to outcome-maximization across contexts, and it leads into conceptual recap because by now the pattern of agreement and hesitation is robust enough to name.

6. First major step back: two moral logics emerge. Sandel explicitly pauses the case sequence and says, in effect, let us notice what our arguments have already revealed: one line of reasoning locates morality in consequences, another in the intrinsic character of the act. This beat appears here because the lecture has finally earned abstraction, and it leads into the course introduction by widening the stakes from these examples to the larger map of moral and political philosophy.

7. Course orientation, stakes, and the warning about philosophy. He broadens from Bentham and Kant to the syllabus, contemporary controversies, the unsettling character of self-knowledge, and the temptation of skepticism. This appears at this point because the lecture has to explain why these stories matter and what kind of inquiry the course will demand, and it leads into the second half by re-situating the opening dilemmas as the beginning of a longer intellectual journey.

8. Internal reset, methodological recap, and Bentham. After the break, Sandel recaps both method and substance: we begin with judgments, articulate principles, then revise them under pressure from new cases; only then does he introduce utilitarianism as a systematic version of consequentialist reasoning. This appears here because Bentham should feel motivated by the earlier cases rather than imposed from outside, and it leads directly into Dudley and Stevens as a real-world stress test of utility.

9. Dudley and Stevens: necessity, arithmetic, and expanding objections. Sandel tells the lifeboat story in concrete detail, then invites the class to act as jury, first allowing necessity and survival arithmetic to make their strongest case, then eliciting objections about murder, altered state of mind, and moral power over another’s life. This appears now because Bentham’s principle needs a harder, less hypothetical test, and it leads into the final phase when the class begins to distinguish necessity from consent and procedure. [Q&A candidate: Is necessity enough to make killing morally permissible?]

10. Lottery, consent, and the three launch questions. He pushes the class from bare survival arithmetic to fair procedure and then to consent, watching more people soften once agreement is imagined, before ending by extracting three precise philosophical questions about rights, procedure, and the moral work of consent. This appears last because the lecture wants to end not with closure but with sharpened problems that point toward the next reading assignment, and it should later become the chapter’s closing hinge into Bentham and Mill. [Q&A candidate: Would a lottery or Parker’s consent make the killing permissible, and if so, why?]

## Transition Cues
- Sandel repeatedly opens a new move with direct prompts such as “here’s our first question,” “let’s take a poll,” or “let’s hear first,” which keep the argument grounded in live judgment.
- When he wants to destabilize a principle, he pivots with a nearby variation: “let’s consider another case,” “this time,” or “suppose.” These are not digressions; they are controlled perturbations.
- He uses explicit meta-pauses to consolidate: “let’s step back” is his signal that the lecture is moving from examples to principle-extraction.
- He marks larger pedagogical pivots with future-looking phrases: “we’re going to explore,” “if you look at the syllabus,” “today and in the next few days,” and “starting next time.”
- The internal break is handled by recap language: “last time” and “we noticed two things” return us to the thread before Bentham is named.
- At the end of the Dudley-Stevens discussion, he uses numbered prompts, “question number one,” “question number two,” “question number three,” to turn a messy conversation into a clean research agenda.

## Recurring Motifs
- One life versus many lives. The lecture keeps returning to small-number moral arithmetic, first as intuitive pressure, then as a limit test for outcome-based reasoning.
- Case variation with one altered feature. Sandel’s rhythm depends on changing setting, role, or mode of agency while keeping enough structure fixed that comparison does real work.
- Poll, then reason, then counterexample. He does not let consensus stand without explanation, and he does not let explanation stand without stress-testing.
- Step back and name the principle. Only after the class has generated the tension does he introduce terms like consequentialist and categorical.
- Philosophy as disturbance. The lecture repeatedly frames inquiry not as information transfer but as making the familiar strange and unsettling inherited certainty.
- Unresolved questions as productive endings. Rather than solving the lecture’s central disputes, Sandel distills them into sharper problems that authorize the next reading.

## Pacing Risks
- A draft writer may compress the early trolley sequence into a generic “trolley problem” summary and lose the crucial rhythm of poll, majority reason, minority objection, then structured variation.
- The fat-man and trap-door exchanges are easy to over-condense, but doing so would erase the felt difficulty Sandel wants us to experience before he names the competing principles.
- The doctor and transplant cases should not be merged into one medical example; the second exists precisely to show that the first numerical intuition does not travel cleanly.
- The “step back” recap around consequentialist versus categorical reasoning is a hinge, not a brief aside. If rushed, the lecture will seem like disconnected anecdotes instead of a guided emergence of concepts.
- The course-warning section about risk, self-knowledge, and skepticism may look expendable, but cutting it would flatten Sandel’s motivation for why these puzzles matter beyond the classroom.
- The post-break recap should not be reduced to repetition. It resets method and prepares Bentham, so it needs to feel like a deliberate re-entry into the argument.
- Dudley and Stevens should not be written as a simple application of utilitarianism. Sandel carefully stages defense, prosecution, consent, lottery, and categorical resistance before extracting the three closing questions.
- The ending must not be over-resolved. Sandel ends by sharpening questions, not by giving a final settled doctrine, and a draft that closes too neatly will lose the lecture’s forward drive.
