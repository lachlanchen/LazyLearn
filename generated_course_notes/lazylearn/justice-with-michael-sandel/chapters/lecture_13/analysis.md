# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc

The lecture opens not with an abstract thesis but with a case: a deaf lesbian couple seeks a deaf sperm donor in order to have a deaf child. Sandel uses that story to force the first boundary question immediately: are we looking at the deliberate creation of a disability, or the preservation of an identity?

From that case he widens the frame by insisting that there will be borderline cases, and he names them in quick succession: deafness, obesity, baldness, orthodontia. This is the lecture’s first major pivot, from one controversial example to a more general classificatory problem about how we distinguish the medically necessary from the merely preferential.

He then states the practical consequence of that boundary problem: the new era of genetic technology requires a public argument about the meaning of health and about the limits of genetic technology. This sentence is the bridge from the opening case to the rest of the lecture and should remain visible as such.

A second pivot then arrives through popular culture. Sandel says that some of the sharpest worries about biotechnology have come through novels and film, and he uses a fictional clip not as ornament but as a way of making consumer-style genetic choice vivid before returning to explicit argument.

From there he moves back into contemporary institutions and markets. California Cryobank becomes the lecture’s first concrete market example: donor traits are cataloged, donors are selected, compensation is specified, and genetic choice begins to look like consumer shopping rather than medical necessity. The lecture’s rhythm here is important: first the market mechanics, then the moral diagnosis.

That diagnosis is then expanded through “designer babies” and the prospect that parents may become responsible for the genetic design of their children. Sandel’s thought moves from market choice to altered family relations: once traits are chosen, parents may be blamed for what their children become.

Only after that buildup does he state the stronger normative claim that consumer uses of genetic engineering are morally impermissible. The final movement then turns institutional: if society does not decide collectively, the market will decide by default. The lecture closes on a fork between market rule and democratic deliberation, and that fork gives the chapter its terminating structure.

## Section Outline

1. **Opening Case: The Deaf Couple and the Deliberately Deaf Child.** Begin with the narrated case exactly as Sandel introduces it, including the search for a deaf sperm donor and the successful birth of a deaf child.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `Is deafness a disability imposed on the child, or a distinctive identity intentionally shared?`

2. **Borderline Cases and the Meaning of Health.** Follow Sandel’s widening move from deafness to obesity, baldness, and orthodontia, preserving the lecture’s momentum from one example to the next.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `When is an intervention medically necessary, and when is it merely preferential?`

3. **Popular Culture as a Diagnostic of Biotechnology.** Keep the explicit pivot to novels and film, using the fictional material to show how consumer-style genetic selection is imagined before it is directly argued about. This section should remain brief and transitional rather than becoming a separate film analysis.

4. **Cryobank, Catalogs, and the Market for Genetic Traits.** Present California Cryobank as Sandel presents it: a for-profit sperm market with donor screening, trait cataloging, and numerical compensation.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `When does reproductive choice become consumer selection?`

5. **Designer Babies and the Expansion of Parental Responsibility.** Move from donor choice to the wider prospect of parents selecting traits such as sex, hair color, eye color, and skin color, then to Sandel’s claim that this would burden parents with a new and damaging responsibility for their children’s traits.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `Would genetic design deepen parental agency, or corrupt the parent-child relation?`

6. **Commodification and Moral Judgment.** Gather the argument’s moral center: children and childbearing become extensions of consumer society, and consumer uses of genetic engineering are therefore morally impermissible. This section should preserve Sandel’s step from descriptive market facts to substantive moral condemnation.

7. **Market or Democracy: Who Decides the Limits?** End where the lecture ends, with the institutional fork between market outcomes and democratic public decision.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `If society does not decide collectively, who decides by default?`
