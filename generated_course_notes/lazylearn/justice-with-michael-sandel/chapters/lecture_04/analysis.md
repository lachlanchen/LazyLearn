# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- Sandel opens with a clean pivot: “Today we turn to John Locke.” He first presents Locke as an apparent ally of libertarianism by emphasizing natural, pre-political rights to life, liberty, and property, and only then asks what those claims require us to imagine about human beings before government exists.
- The first major transition is from natural rights to the state of nature. Sandel introduces the state of nature not as chaos but as a conceptual device for understanding what a natural right would mean before legislatures, courts, and political institutions come on the scene.
- Once the state of nature is in place, Sandel immediately complicates it: it is a state of liberty, but not a state of license. This is the lecture’s first important formal distinction, and it leads directly to the question of what constrains action in the absence of positive law.
- He then asks where that constraint comes from and gives two answers in sequence: first God’s prior property right in us, then reason as the law of nature. The lecture thus moves from theological grounding to a more philosophical one without dropping the first.
- From there Sandel pivots to the notion of unalienable rights. The airline-ticket analogy and the Declaration of Independence are not digressions; they are the devices by which he clarifies the paradox that rights can be “more deeply mine” precisely because they are not fully disposable.
- The next transition is from rights in general to property in particular. Sandel frames the property question exactly as Locke does: how can private property arise even before government? This ushers in the lecture’s central constructive chain from self-possession to labor, from labor to mixing, and from mixing to appropriation, with the proviso that enough and as good be left for others.
- Having built Locke’s labor theory of appropriation, Sandel tests it twice. First he uses a contemporary dispute over drug patents to keep the intuition of labor-creation alive in a modern setting; then he turns to the classroom objections about Native Americans, colonization, common use, and enclosure to pressure the argument historically and morally.
- The lecture then makes a second major pivot: from property to consent. There is a broadcast-style splice around 00:25:14--00:25:49, where a later exchange about taxes appears just before a recap (“Last time…”). In the notes this should be treated as an internal reset or teaser, not as the true starting point of the consent argument.
- In the consent portion, Sandel moves back to the state of nature and asks why anyone would leave it. The answer is the “inconveniences” of universal private enforcement: everyone is an executor of the law of nature, punishment overshoots, insecurity spreads, and consent becomes the mechanism for creating civil society and majority rule.
- Once civil society is formed, Sandel sharpens the deepest puzzle of the lecture: if natural rights remain in force, what exactly can majority rule authorize? This sets up the crucial textual tension in Locke’s discussion of taxation and property, especially the movement from “no taking without consent” to “consent of the majority.”
- The final stretch of the lecture tests that puzzle through cases: taxation, implied consent, withdrawal from society, use of public goods, and military conscription. Sandel then proposes Locke’s likely escape route: the real target is arbitrary power, while general laws enacted by fair procedures do not count as the same kind of violation.
- The lecture ends by returning to the larger philosophical diagnosis. Locke disappoints libertarians twice: first because rights are unalienable and therefore self-ownership is not absolute, and second because legitimate government can still do quite a lot so long as it acts through non-arbitrary law. Sandel closes by displacing the problem forward: if consent is neither nothing nor everything, what work can it really do, especially in markets?

## Section Outline
1. **Locke as the Apparent Libertarian Ally.** Open with Sandel’s initial framing: Locke seems to support the libertarian by affirming pre-political natural rights to life, liberty, and property and by treating property as a natural right rather than a creation of government.

2. **State of Nature: Liberty, Not License.** Explain why Sandel moves from natural rights to the state of nature, and preserve the key distinction between liberty and license. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: If we are free and equal in the state of nature, why are we not free to do whatever we want?`

3. **Unalienable Rights and Their Two Foundations.** Present Locke’s two answers to the source of constraint: first God’s prior property right in us, then reason as the law of nature. Keep the paradox of unalienability explicit: in one sense non-transferability limits ownership, and in another it makes rights more profoundly ours.

4. **Property Before Government: From Person to Labor to Land.** Unfold the labor-mixing argument step by step from self-possession to labor, from labor to appropriation, and from appropriation to ownership of both products and land, keeping the “enough and as good” proviso in view. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: How can private property arise before government or consent?`

5. **Testing Locke’s Property Argument: Patents, Colonization, and Common Use.** Use the patent dispute and then the classroom discussion of Native Americans and colonial appropriation to show Sandel pressure-testing Locke’s account rather than merely stating it. This section should preserve the lecture’s movement from intuition pump to historical objection.

6. **Consent as the Second Big Idea: Leaving the State of Nature.** Explain the “inconveniences” of the state of nature, especially universal private enforcement, and then show how consent creates civil society, legislature, and majority rule without supposedly erasing natural rights. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: If natural rights remain in force, what exactly does consent authorize the majority to do?`

7. **Taxation, Implied Consent, Conscription, and Locke’s Limits.** Track the lecture’s second-half cases in order: property as natural yet conventionally defined, taxation by majority consent, implied consent for those born into society, the possibility of withdrawal, and the conscription puzzle. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Can those who never explicitly consented still be bound by government, taxation, or even conscription?`

## Mathematical Content To Include
- [transcript-backed] The lecture’s first sharp distinction can be rendered schematically as
  \[
  \text{State of Nature} = \text{Liberty} \neq \text{License}.
  \]
  This is not visual board notation, but it captures Sandel’s explicit verbal distinction.

- [standard reconstruction] A compact summary of Locke’s basic natural-rights set:
  \[
  R_{\text{natural}}=\{\text{life},\ \text{liberty},\ \text{property}\}.
  \]

- [transcript-backed] The law-of-nature constraint should be represented conceptually as a pair of prohibitions:
  \[
  \text{may not alienate one’s own natural rights}, \qquad
  \text{may not violate another’s natural rights}.
  \]
  This is directly backed by the transcript’s repeated “nor can we take them from somebody else” / “nor am I free to take my own life…”

- [standard reconstruction] Locke’s first answer can be written as an ordered-right claim:
  \[
  \text{God’s prior property right in us} \succ \text{our discretionary power over ourselves}.
  \]
  This should be labeled as editorial shorthand for the “workmanship of God” argument.

- [transcript-backed] Locke’s second answer, reason as law, can be summarized as
  \[
  \text{Reason} = \text{Law of Nature},
  \qquad
  \forall x\neq y,\ x \text{ ought not harm } y \text{ in life, health, liberty, or possessions}.
  \]
  The universal quantifier is editorial, but the substance is transcript-backed.

- [transcript-backed] The lecture’s core chain for property should appear explicitly:
  \[
  \text{property in person} \to \text{property in labor} \to \text{mix labor with unowned things} \to \text{private property}.
  \]

- [transcript-backed] The stronger land-appropriation version should be preserved as a separate claim:
  \[
  \text{till}+\text{plant}+\text{improve}+\text{cultivate}
  \Longrightarrow
  \text{property not only in crop but in land}.
  \]

- [transcript-backed] Locke’s proviso should be displayed in compact form:
  \[
  \text{appropriation is valid only if there is “enough and as good” left for others}.
  \]
  The phrase should remain close to Locke’s wording.

- [standard reconstruction] The state-of-nature-to-consent transition is best shown as a logical chain:
  \[
  \text{everyone enforces law of nature}
  \to
  \text{punishment overshoots}
  \to
  \text{insecurity}
  \to
  \text{consent to civil society}.
  \]

- [transcript-backed] The consent move should be represented as giving up enforcement power rather than natural rights:
  \[
  \text{consent} \Longrightarrow \text{surrender private enforcement power},
  \qquad
  \text{not} \Longrightarrow \text{surrender natural rights}.
  \]

- [transcript-backed] The crucial tax-consent tension from sections 138 and 140 should be preserved in quasi-formal contrast:
  \[
  \text{no taking of property without consent}
  \]
  alongside
  \[
  \text{consent relevant to taxation} = \text{consent of the majority}.
  \]
  This is one of the lecture’s main logical pressure points.

- [transcript-backed] Sandel’s synthesis that property is natural in one sense and conventional in another should be made explicit:
  \[
  \text{property as an institution is natural}, \qquad
  \text{what counts as mine is conventionally defined}.
  \]

- [standard reconstruction] The likely Lockean answer to the later puzzle should be written as
  \[
  \text{arbitrary taking} = \text{illegitimate},
  \qquad
  \text{general non-arbitrary law} \neq \text{arbitrary taking}.
  \]
  This is not a quoted formula, but it captures Sandel’s reconstruction of Locke near the end.

- [transcript-backed] The final disappointment-for-libertarians contrast can be compressed as
  \[
  \text{unalienable rights} \Longrightarrow \text{self-ownership is not absolute},
  \]
  and
  \[
  \text{consent}+\text{general law} \Longrightarrow \text{government may be less limited than libertarians hope}.
  \]
