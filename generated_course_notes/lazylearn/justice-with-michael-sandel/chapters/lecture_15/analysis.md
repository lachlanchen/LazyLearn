# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc

The lecture opens not with a theory but with a poll question displayed on screen: is it fair that Wayne Rooney earns more than a care worker? Sandel begins by forcing a binary judgment before any argument is heard, so the chapter should preserve that sequence: first the question, then the vote, and only then the reasons.

He next reports the poll result in numerical form, emphasizing that the room is ``pretty well divided'': `46\%` say fair, `54\%` say unfair. This is the lecture's first mathematical move, and it matters because Sandel uses the near-even split to motivate discussion rather than to settle anything.

The discussion then proceeds dialectically. Sandel first invites a defense of the claim that Rooney's higher pay is fair, and the first response grounds fairness in the service Rooney provides, the pleasure people derive from it, and their willingness to pay for it. That argument then gets refined into scarcity: very few people can do what Rooney does.

Only after that market-facing defense is on the table does Sandel ask for disagreement. Lois shifts the frame from willingness to pay to equal life chances and social value, arguing that care work sustains life in a way entertainment does not. This is the lecture's second major pivot, from market valuation to moral valuation.

Sandel then presses Lois by asking whether the care worker contributes more to society. That question matters because it forces the anti-market side to decide whether the issue is unequal opportunity, social importance, or both. Paul responds by making the economist's move explicit: supply and demand explain the wage difference, because many can do care work and few can do what Rooney does.

The final pivot belongs to Susan. She grants that supply and demand may explain how wages are set, but denies that this explains what is fair. The lecture closes this segment by bringing the core conceptual distinction into focus: market price and moral desert may come apart. That distinction should anchor the chapter ending.

## Section Outline

1. **Opening Poll: Fair or Unfair?** Begin with the displayed question about Rooney and the care worker, preserving the lecture's opening as a poll before it becomes an argument.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `What exactly is the question being asked here: market explanation, moral fairness, or social value?`

2. **A Divided Room: The First Numerical Split.** Report the `46\%` versus `54\%` result as Sandel does, stressing that the close division motivates discussion rather than resolves it. This section should make the vote count the lecture's first explicit numerical structure.

3. **Paul's Market Defense of Rooney's Pay.** Present the first pro-fairness argument in transcript order: Rooney provides pleasure, people willingly pay for it, very few others can do it, and therefore he earns a lot.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `Does willingness to pay for a scarce talent show that the resulting wage is fair?`

4. **Lois: Equal Chance and Social Value.** Follow the first major objection by shifting from consumer willingness to pay toward equal opportunity and the life-sustaining importance of care work.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `If people did not begin with equal chances in life, can pay differences still count as fair?`

5. **Paul's Economist Reply: Supply and Demand.** Let Sandel return to Paul so that the defense becomes sharper and more formal: many people can provide care services, far fewer have Rooney's rare talent, and wages track scarcity. This is where the chapter should show the strongest economic argument on its own terms.

6. **Susan and the Distinction Between Price and Fairness.** End with Susan's reply that the laws of supply and demand may govern wages without defining what is fair, then let Sandel crystallize that distinction.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `Do market prices measure fairness, or only the outcome of supply and demand?`
