# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc

The lecture opens with a practical cognitive puzzle, not with a framework definition. We begin from the felt experience of being put on the spot: the brain panics, opens “a dozen browser tabs,” and rambling replaces clear points. The first movement is therefore diagnostic and motivational: why does spontaneous speaking break down, and why does it feel so bad?

From that diagnosis, Zhang inserts personal testimony and then pivots into solution mode through a workshop clip. The key transition is the confidence contrast: prepared content feels manageable; off-the-cuff speaking feels unstable. That leads directly to the sharp question, “what do you fall back on?” and the answer “generally, rambling,” which then motivates the need for frameworks.

Only then does he introduce the core object of the lecture: the `3,2,1` framework. He names it, praises its usefulness, states where he uses it, and then immediately defines it twice in close succession: three steps, two types, one thing. This repetition is part of the lecture’s rhythm and should remain visible in the notes.

The next move is demonstrative rather than abstract. Zhang stress-tests the framework on a random topic, “avocado,” and deliberately begins with the easiest entry point, the “one thing,” before expanding to “two ways/types” and “three steps.” The pedagogical point is not just what the scaffold is, but how it gives the mind “something to lean on” and prevents uncontrolled topic branching.

From there he pivots from teacher demonstration to live audience use. The lecture briefly interrupts itself with a promotional aside about other communication frameworks; this should be kept only insofar as it clarifies that `3,2,1` belongs to a broader family of speaking structures. The accepted `Visibility` frame supports that wider placement, but it should remain secondary.

The final movement returns to the workshop and resolves the main tension with a student demonstration on the topic “travel.” The student succeeds quickly with one thing, two types, and three steps, and Zhang closes by interpreting that success psychologically: frameworks do not merely organize content; they change bodily readiness, confidence, and the willingness to lean into communication.

## Section Outline

1. **Opening Problem: Why We Ramble Under Pressure.** Begin with the panic-button/browser-tabs diagnosis and the embarrassment/confidence consequences. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: “Why do we ramble when we are put on the spot?”

2. **From Rambling to Frameworks.** Preserve the confidence contrast, the workshop pivot, and the decisive question “what do you fall back on?” This section should end by introducing frameworks as the replacement for rambling.

3. **The `3,2,1` Framework Defined.** State the framework exactly in transcript order as “three steps, two types, one thing,” while also noting that Zhang can enter it from whichever slot is easiest in practice. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: “What exactly does `3,2,1` mean?”

4. **Worked Example: Avocado and the Mind’s Fallback Structure.** Walk through Zhang’s use of “one thing,” then “two ways/types,” then “three steps,” and contrast that with the chaotic unstructured thought stream about avocados. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: “How does the framework give the brain something to lean on?”

5. **Live Pressure Drill: Teaching the Student to Use the Scaffold.** Preserve the transition from self-demo to audience exercise, the three-second pressure setup, and the eventual “travel” responses. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: “Why does a framework change not only what we say, but how ready we feel?”

6. **Beyond `3,2,1`: Other Frameworks and the Closing Lesson.** Keep the promotional aside brief but concrete, using it to show that `3,2,1` is one item in a wider framework toolkit. Close with Zhang’s final contrast between scared communication and framework-backed, excited communication.
