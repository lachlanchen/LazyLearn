# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
Sandel opens by explicitly returning to Locke and to the unresolved question from the previous lecture: if government rests on consent, what limits remain on what the majority may authorize? He first sharpens the issue by moving from taxation to life itself. Taxation already showed that individual contemporaneous consent is not required once one has entered political society; the harder case is conscription, because it seems to conflict with the idea that we own ourselves.

The first major transition comes when Sandel gives Locke’s answer. Conscription is compatible with legitimate government, he says, so long as military authority is not arbitrary. The famous sergeant example then functions as a moral stress test: command over life in war may be legitimate, while even taking a penny outside rightful authority would be corrupt. This recasts the Lockean limit as a distinction between arbitrary and non-arbitrary power.

Sandel then pivots from Locke’s political theory to a contemporary policy problem: the United States is struggling to meet recruiting targets in Iraq. He introduces three possible responses in sequence, first higher pay, then conscription by lottery, then outsourcing to mercenaries. The accepted slide gives visual evidence for the first two options, while the transcript supplies the third. This is the lecture’s first concrete decision tree.

From there the lecture becomes dialectical. Sandel polls the room, introduces the Civil War hybrid system of draft plus buyout, and uses the classroom exchange to uncover the first objection to markets in military service: background inequality can make seemingly voluntary exchange partly coercive. He then immediately allows a counterpressure: if that coercion objection troubles us in the Civil War case, should it not also trouble us in the all-volunteer army?

The next transition is subtle but important. Having heard the coercion objection, Sandel lets Emily push the argument further: perhaps the all-volunteer military simply universalizes the morally troubling feature of the Civil War buyout system. That move then opens a second axis of criticism, not about coercion but about civic meaning. If military service is bound up with patriotism and civic obligation, perhaps it should not be treated as just another wage labor market.

Sandel then steps back and performs the lecture’s first explicit synthesis. We have not merely heard scattered opinions; we have heard two broad objections to market allocation in military service. First, coercion against a background of inequality. Second, corruption or misvaluation: military service may be the wrong kind of good to allocate by market exchange. He formulates the two questions these objections raise and then deliberately leaves them open.

A brief teaser about bidding for a baby serves as the bridge to the second half. Sandel then shifts from military service to human reproduction, first through egg markets and sperm markets, then through the Baby M surrogacy case. The shift is not a digression: it transports the same consent-versus-market logic into a more intimate domain where the moral stakes are sharper.

The surrogacy discussion unfolds in the same rhythm as the military discussion. Sandel first invites a contractarian defense: a deal is a deal, and voluntary informed agreement should be enforced. He then lets the objections emerge from the classroom: perhaps the consent was not fully informed, because the surrogate mother could not know in advance the force of the maternal bond; perhaps the very object of exchange is misdescribed, because this is too close to baby selling or to the sale of a mother’s right to her child.

The final transition is from the classroom debate to the court’s reasoning, and then from the court to philosophical abstraction. The New Jersey Supreme Court rejects enforceability for two kinds of reasons: flawed consent and commodification. Sandel then connects the second line of argument to Elizabeth Anderson’s idea of alienated labor and to the more general claim that some goods are properly valued by respect, love, honor, awe, or sanctity rather than by use and price. The lecture ends by widening the field rather than resolving it: what goods, if any, should money not buy?

## Section Outline
1. **From Locke to Conscription.** Open with Sandel’s recap from the previous lecture: taxation has already shown that political obligation does not require ongoing individual consent, and now the harder case is whether the state may conscript citizens to risk their lives. This section should preserve the movement from property to life and from self-possession to military service.

2. **Arbitrary Power and the Lockean Limit.** Present Locke’s answer through the sergeant example and Sandel’s emphasis on non-arbitrary authority. `Question & Answer` should appear here: `Question & Answer: If we own ourselves, how can government ever command us to fight?`

3. **Recruitment Options and the Civil War Hybrid.** Introduce the Iraq recruitment problem, the three policy options, and the Civil War draft-plus-buyout system. Keep the accepted recruitment slide visible here, and use it to anchor the lecture’s initial comparison before expanding from the slide’s two visible options to the transcript’s full three-option sequence.

4. **Coercion, Inequality, and the All-Volunteer Army.** Follow the classroom exchange from Liz to Sam and Raul to Emily, preserving how the lecture turns from “putting a price on life” to the claim that background inequality can corrupt apparently free exchange. `Question & Answer` should appear here: `Question & Answer: When does a voluntary exchange stop being fully free because inequality is doing the work?`

5. **Patriotism, Civic Obligation, and Market Allocation.** Track the next turn from coercion to the question of civic meaning: should military service be motivated by patriotism rather than pay, and if so, does that support conscription over a paid army? End this section with Sandel’s synthesis of the lecture’s two objections to markets in military service.

6. **From Military Service to Markets in Reproduction.** Mark the bridge from Andrew’s challenge about bidding for a baby to the markets in eggs and sperm, and then to Baby M. The aim is to preserve Sandel’s move from one domain of contested market exchange to another without making the transition feel abrupt.

7. **Surrogacy, Contract, and the Two Objections Revisited.** Present the Baby M contract, the initial contractarian defense, the objections from lack of information and from dehumanization, and then the court’s two-part reasoning. `Question & Answer` should appear here: `Question & Answer: Can a surrogacy contract be fully voluntary if the mother cannot yet know the bond she will form with the child?`

8. **Commodification and the Limits of Market Reason.** Close with Sandel’s turn to Elizabeth Anderson, alienated labor, and the claim that some goods are properly valued in ways other than use. End by leaving the lecture open where Sandel leaves it: the question is not yet settled, but the problem of what money should and should not buy has now been clearly posed.

The notes should preserve only the stable logical structure there and not over-interpret broken fragments.
- No literal equations or blackboard mathematics appear in the accepted frames. Any displayed formulas in the chapter will therefore be editorially introduced schematic notation, useful for sharpening the lecture’s structure but not visually recovered mathematics.
- The lecture’s “mathematical spine” is conceptual rather than numerical. It lies in distinctions, branching alternatives, and paired objections, not in algebraic derivation. The notes should therefore use equations sparingly and only where they clarify structure.
- The all-volunteer military versus Civil War buyout comparison is rhetorically cumulative and should not be flattened too quickly into a static taxonomy. The lecture’s force comes from the way one objection mutates into the next through student replies.
- The surrogacy section should preserve the distinction between two different criticisms:
  first, that consent may be flawed;
  second, that some goods may be degraded when treated as market commodities.
  These should not be collapsed into a single generic complaint about unfairness.
