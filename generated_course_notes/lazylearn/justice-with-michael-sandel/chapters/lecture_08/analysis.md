# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- Sandel opens by explicitly turning to distributive justice and by recapping the contractual machinery built in the previous lecture: principles of justice are to be chosen from behind the veil of ignorance in an original position of equality. The lecture therefore begins not from Rawls's conclusions, but from the setup that is supposed to justify them.
- He first asks what principles would be chosen in that original position and starts by ruling out a major rival, utilitarianism. The pivot is motivational and practical: behind the veil, no one will risk becoming an oppressed minority whose rights are traded away for aggregate welfare.
- From that rejection he derives the first principle, equal basic liberties, and then shifts to the second principle by asking what to do about social and economic inequalities. The movement is deliberate: liberties first, then income, wealth, and opportunity.
- Sandel initially stages the most cautious answer to inequality, strict equality, only to overturn it almost immediately. The transition is that even the least advantaged could do better under a qualified principle of inequality, and this motivates the difference principle.
- He then stress-tests the difference principle with familiar salary examples like Michael Jordan and Bill Gates. These examples are not mere illustrations; they motivate the question of what sort of institutional arrangement would make inequalities acceptable.
- The lecture next becomes dialogical. Mike challenges Rawls from behind the veil, arguing for a merit-based system that rewards effort, and Kate counters by stressing the unequal social advantages that shape educational success. This exchange shifts the lecture from Rawls's “official” contract argument to a second, more straightforwardly moral argument about arbitrariness.
- Sandel then unfolds a ladder of rival distributive regimes in historical and conceptual order: feudal aristocracy, formal equality of opportunity, fair equality of opportunity, meritocracy, and finally Rawls's more egalitarian democratic conception. The race metaphor is the lecture's main organizing device in this middle stretch.
- The key transition here is that each new view corrects one arbitrariness while leaving another in place. Birth-based hierarchy yields to open competition; open competition still leaves unequal starting points; equal starting points still leave the natural lottery untouched.
- Having reached the difference principle through the race metaphor, Sandel returns to concrete pay differentials and then to objections. The transcript appears to contain a reset/recap seam in the later middle of the lecture, where earlier material is re-entered and reorganized before the objections are developed.
- The objections are ordered carefully. First comes the incentives objection, which Sandel treats as serious but manageable; then come the two deeper objections, from meritocratic effort/desert and from libertarian self-ownership.
- Sandel answers the incentives objection by narrowing the relevant metric: not the size of the total economic pie, but the effect of incentives and disincentives on those at the bottom. This keeps the difference principle intact while allowing qualified inequalities.
- He answers the libertarian objection by insisting that the natural distribution of talents is neither just nor unjust; what matters is how institutions respond to it. This reframes the dispute from “correcting nature” to structuring institutions.
- He answers the meritocratic objection in two stages: first by arguing that even work ethic and striving depend on contingencies such as family background and even birth order, and second by arguing that defenders of meritocracy do not really reward effort as such, but contribution.
- The lecture culminates in Rawls's distinction between moral desert and entitlements to legitimate expectations, illustrated by games of chance and games of skill. This is the final conceptual pivot, and it lets Sandel carry the discussion from income and wealth to honors and opportunities, especially access to elite colleges and universities.
- The ending is deliberately forward-looking. Rawls's question is extended to admissions and affirmative action, which means the chapter should close not with a flattened summary, but with the live problem the lecture leaves for next time.

## Section Outline
1. **From the Original Position to the First Principle.** Sandel opens by recalling the veil of ignorance and asking what principles would actually be chosen there.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Why would parties behind the veil reject utilitarianism?`

2. **From Strict Equality to the Difference Principle.** The lecture then turns to income and wealth, first entertaining strict equality and then replacing it with Rawls's qualified principle that allows inequality only when it helps the least well-off.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Why would rational choosers accept some inequality rather than insist on equal shares?`

3. **Merit, Family Background, and the Classroom Challenge.** Mike's merit-based objection and Kate's response push the lecture from the contract argument to the deeper claim that distributive shares should not rest on morally arbitrary contingencies.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Why not choose a meritocratic system behind the veil of ignorance?`

4. **The Ladder of Rival Theories.** Sandel reconstructs Rawls's sequence from feudal aristocracy to formal equality, fair equality of opportunity, meritocracy, and finally the democratic conception defined by the difference principle.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Why is formal equality of opportunity, and even fair equality of opportunity, still not enough?`

5. **Natural Lottery, Incentives, and the Least Well-Off.** The lecture then clarifies that Rawls need not endorse flat equality of outcome; incentives may be allowed if they improve the position of those at the bottom.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Can the difference principle allow incentives without collapsing into laissez-faire?`

6. **Self-Ownership and the Libertarian Objection.** Sandel next takes up the libertarian challenge from self-ownership and replies by shifting the focus from natural facts to the justice of institutions that respond to them.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: If talents are mine, why may society tax the gains that flow from them?`

7. **Effort, Contribution, and Moral Desert.** The lecture culminates in Rawls's distinction between moral desert and entitlements to legitimate expectations, using lotteries, baseball, and labor examples to show why distributive justice is not fundamentally about who deserves more.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Is distributive justice about moral desert, or only about what institutions entitle us to expect?`

8. **From Income to Educational Opportunity.** Sandel closes by extending the same Rawlsian logic from earnings to honors and access, asking what moral claim students at elite universities have on the opportunities they enjoy.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Are elite educational opportunities rewards for desert, or entitlements that require public justification?`
