# Narrative Map
## Opening Setup
Sandel opens by telling us exactly where we are in the larger Rawls sequence: last time established why principles of justice should be chosen from behind a veil of ignorance, and now we will ask which principles would actually be chosen there. The setup matters because the lecture does not begin with a list of doctrines; it begins with a device for choosing them, and every later move is presented as something we can see for ourselves once we enter that hypothetical standpoint.

He also frames the whole hour with a broad distributive question: how income, wealth, power, and opportunity should be distributed, and according to what principles. That gives the lecture a steady forward drive: from setup, to principles, to objections, to a closing extension from money to educational opportunity.

## Beat Sequence
1. **From recap to choice of principles.** Sandel first reactivates the machinery of the original position and veil of ignorance, because he wants the audience to feel that the next conclusions are generated from that setup rather than merely asserted. This appears at the start to re-anchor the argument in the previous lecture and leads directly into the first concrete test: which rival principle would we reject first?  
   `Question & Answer` beat: Why would parties behind the veil reject utilitarianism?

2. **Rejecting utilitarianism to secure the first principle.** He rules out utilitarianism by asking us to imagine winding up as a minority whose liberty could be traded away for aggregate welfare; the motivational force is fear of oppression and the desire for dignity. This comes early because it clears conceptual space for Rawls’s first principle, equal basic liberties, and then opens the way to the harder question of economic inequality.

3. **From strict equality to the difference principle.** Sandel next turns to the second principle by first staging the simplest safe answer, equal shares of income and wealth, and then showing why even the least fortunate might rationally prefer a qualified inequality. This appears here because the lecture needs to move from liberty to distribution, and it leads naturally into the formulation of the difference principle as the criterion for acceptable inequality.  
   `Question & Answer` beat: Why would rational choosers allow some inequality rather than insist on equal distribution?

4. **Testing Rawls with famous inequalities.** Michael Jordan and Bill Gates enter as live stress tests: if Rawls allows inequality, what kinds, under what institutional terms, and why? These examples come immediately after the principle because Sandel wants to keep the theory from floating free of ordinary judgment, and they lead into classroom resistance about whether Rawls’s two principles would really be chosen.

5. **Mike’s challenge and Kate’s counterchallenge.** The lecture becomes dialectical when Mike argues for a merit-based system and Kate presses the point that effort and achievement are socially conditioned by unequal family and educational advantages. This appears at exactly the right moment because it forces Sandel to widen the frame from the “official” veil-of-ignorance argument to a second, more moral argument about arbitrariness, and it leads into Rawls’s taxonomy of rival distributive schemes.  
   `Question & Answer` beat: Why not choose meritocracy behind the veil of ignorance?

6. **The ladder of rival theories and the race metaphor.** Sandel then walks us step by step from feudal aristocracy to formal equality of opportunity, then to fair equality of opportunity, then to meritocracy, and finally beyond meritocracy to the democratic conception defined by the difference principle. This beat appears here because the classroom exchange has exposed the need to distinguish different kinds of unfairness, and it leads into Rawls’s deeper claim that even equal starting points do not neutralize the natural lottery.  
   `Question & Answer` beat: Why are formal equality and even fair equality of opportunity still insufficient?

7. **Beyond leveling equality: changing the terms of reward.** Once the natural lottery is on the table, Sandel pauses over the obvious worry that the only alternative would be crude leveling or handicapping the talented; he rejects that worry by saying Rawls does not oppose talent cultivation, only unrestricted entitlement to its market fruits. This beat matters because it keeps the lecture from collapsing into caricature, and it sets up the concrete pay-differential examples as ways of seeing how the difference principle would actually operate.

8. **Concrete salaries, then a visible recap seam.** The teacher–Letterman and O’Connor–Judge Judy comparisons translate the difference principle into institutional questions about taxation and the basic structure of society, but the transcript then visibly resets into a recap of the earlier ladder and race argument. That re-entry appears awkward on the page but is pedagogically useful: it re-gathers the main alternatives before Sandel turns to objections, so the next movement has a cleaner target.

9. **Objections in ascending depth.** Sandel takes the incentives objection first because it is the most practical and most familiar: if we tax too much, won’t the talented stop producing? He answers it by shifting the metric from the total size of the pie to the effect on the least well-off, which preserves the difference principle while allowing incentives, and this clears the way for the two harder objections, from self-ownership and from effort/desert.  
   `Question & Answer` beat: Can the difference principle allow incentives without collapsing into laissez-faire?

10. **From self-ownership and effort to moral desert versus entitlement.** The lecture’s last major movement first answers the libertarian objection by distinguishing natural facts from the justice of institutions, then answers the meritocratic objection by showing that effort is itself contingent and that defenders of meritocracy really care about contribution, not effort as such. This culminates in Rawls’s distinction between moral desert and entitlements to legitimate expectations, illustrated through lottery and game-of-skill examples, and it leads directly into the closing extension from income to honors and college admissions.  
   `Question & Answer` beat: Is distributive justice about moral desert, or about what institutions legitimately entitle us to expect?

## Transition Cues
- Sandel begins with recap language that does real work: “we put ourselves in a position to do so last time,” followed by “all right, then let’s turn...” The effect is to make the lecture feel cumulative rather than freshly restarted.
- He often pivots by posing a rival view as a live option: “What about utilitarianism?” “What would we agree to?” “Why not the top?” This keeps the exposition from hardening into summary.
- He likes to stage a provisional answer only to revise it: “we might at first thought say...” followed by “but then we would realize...” That rhythm is essential to the lecture’s feel and should survive in the notes.
- Classroom objections are absorbed rather than treated as interruptions. He names the student, restates the challenge, and then broadens it into the next conceptual move.
- The race metaphor is introduced as an intuitive check, not as a decorative analogy. “If you let everybody run the race...” becomes the bridge from formal equality to fair equality and then beyond meritocracy.
- When objections begin, Sandel explicitly escalates their seriousness: incentives first, then “two weightier, more difficult objections.” That ordering should be preserved.
- Late in the lecture he uses “Now...” and “I want to turn now...” to mark major argumentative resets. Those pivots matter because they tell us which objection is being answered and why.
- The distinction between desert and entitlement is introduced with almost textbook care: he slows down, gives contrasting games, and then draws the moral conclusion. That beat should feel like a deliberate conceptual landing, not a passing aside.

## Recurring Motifs
- **The veil as a test of fairness.** Sandel repeatedly returns to what we would choose if we did not know where we would land.
- **The least well-off as the decisive perspective.** Again and again he asks not what enlarges the aggregate pie, but what improves the position of those at the bottom.
- **Moral arbitrariness.** Birth, family background, educational advantage, natural talent, work ethic, and social demand all reappear as unchosen contingencies.
- **The race metaphor.** Unequal starting points, equal starting lines, fast and slow runners, and the temptation to handicap the gifted organize the lecture’s middle logic.
- **Concrete pay comparisons.** Jordan, Gates, Letterman, O’Connor, and Judge Judy keep pulling the argument back into recognizable social facts.
- **Institutional rather than natural justice.** Rawls’s refrain is that nature itself is neither just nor unjust; the real question is what institutions do with natural facts.
- **Desert versus entitlement.** The lecture’s last phase is governed by the contrast between what the rules entitle us to and what we may think we morally deserve.

## Pacing Risks
- A draft is very likely to compress the opening too fast and jump straight to the two principles, losing Sandel’s insistence that the principles are generated from the original position rather than merely listed.
- The move from strict equality to the difference principle can be flattened into a slogan; the notes should preserve the intermediate moment where equal shares look safest before being displaced by the thought that the worst off might do better under qualified inequality.
- Mike and Kate should not be reduced to decorative classroom color. Their exchange is the hinge that moves the lecture from the official contract argument to the deeper moral-arbitrariness argument.
- The ladder from aristocracy to meritocracy is easy to summarize too abstractly. The lecture’s force comes from showing that each view corrects one arbitrariness while leaving another untouched.
- The middle transcript reset around the salary examples and the later recap is easy to “fix” away in drafting, but doing so would erase the lecture’s actual rhythm. It is better to mark it as a re-entry or recap seam.
- The incentives objection should not be allowed to dominate the whole lecture simply because it is vivid and practical. Sandel clearly treats it as important but not decisive, before turning to the deeper objections.
- The self-ownership objection and the effort/desert objection should not be merged into one generic anti-Rawls complaint. Sandel separates them carefully and answers them differently.
- The desert/entitlement distinction is the point where many drafts will become too compressed or too textbook-like. The lottery and game-of-skill examples should remain because they slow the lecture down in exactly the right place.
- The final move to college admissions and affirmative action should remain open-ended. If the draft resolves it too neatly, it will lose the lecture’s closing tension and its invitation to the next chapter.
