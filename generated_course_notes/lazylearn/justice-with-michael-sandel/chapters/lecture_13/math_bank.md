## Definitions And Objects

- `deafness`
  The opening contested case; treated in the lecture as the central test of how we classify a trait.

- `disability`
  One side of the opening classificatory fork; implies impairment and possible harm to the child.

- `distinctive identity`
  The opposing side of the opening fork; implies a mode of life or shared human identity rather than a defect.

- `medically necessary`
  The category for interventions justified by health or treatment rather than preference.

- `purely preferential`
  The category for interventions chosen for desire, convenience, or taste rather than medical need.

- `meaning of health`
  The public concept Sandel says genetic technology forces us to debate.

- `limits of genetic technology`
  The second public concept paired with the meaning of health.

- `California Cryobank`
  The concrete market example through which Sandel shifts from abstract biotechnology to commercial reproductive choice.

- `cataloged donor traits`
  The package of donor information sold in the market example: physical traits, ethnic origin, college major, and related characteristics.

- `consumer uses of genetic engineering`
  Sandel’s term for non-therapeutic, choice-driven applications of biotechnology.

- `commodification`
  The transformation of children and childbearing into market goods or consumer objects.

- `explosion of parental responsibility`
  Sandel’s phrase for the increase in accountability parents would bear once traits become selectable.

- `market`
  One of the two agencies that can decide by default if there is no collective public determination.

- `democratic institutions`
  The other agency, associated with public debate and shared ethical decision.

## Derivation Steps

1. Opening case to boundary question
   1. A couple wants a child.
   2. They want the child to be born deaf.
   3. They choose a deaf donor to make that outcome more likely.
   4. Therefore the case is not accidental misfortune but intentional trait selection.
   5. This generates the fork: disability or identity.

2. Single case to general classificatory problem
   1. Deafness is introduced as the opening hard case.
   2. Sandel then adds obesity, baldness, and orthodontia.
   3. These examples widen the field from one controversial case to a class of borderline interventions.
   4. Therefore the core issue becomes classification:
      \[
      \text{medically necessary} \quad \text{vs.} \quad \text{purely preferential}.
      \]

3. Boundary problem to public debate
   1. Borderline cases cannot be resolved by a fixed technical rule in the lecture.
   2. They require judgments about what counts as health.
   3. Judgments about health set judgments about permissible technological intervention.
   4. Therefore genetic technology requires public debate about both health and limits.

4. Market example to commodification
   1. Donor sperm is screened and cataloged by traits.
   2. Consumers choose among trait profiles.
   3. Compensation is paid and advertised.
   4. Reproduction begins to resemble a market transaction.
   5. Therefore childbearing is drawn into consumer society.

5. Consumer choice to altered family relation
   1. Genetic technology expands beyond therapy toward trait selection.
   2. Selected traits become attributable to parental choice.
   3. Children may then regard parents as responsible for chosen attributes.
   4. This increases parental responsibility beyond ordinary parenthood.
   5. Therefore the parent-child relation risks becoming damaged and commodified.

6. Moral conclusion to institutional fork
   1. Consumer uses of genetic engineering commodify children.
   2. Sandel judges those uses morally impermissible.
   3. A society must still decide whether to permit or limit them.
   4. If there is no public collective decision, market forces decide in practice.
   5. Therefore the final institutional fork is market versus democratic institutions.
