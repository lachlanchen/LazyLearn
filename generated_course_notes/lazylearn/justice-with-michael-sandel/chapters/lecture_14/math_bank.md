## Definitions And Objects

- `transplant surgeon`
  The role assigned to the audience; the agent of the thought experiment.

- `five patients`
  The aggregate set of urgent claimants, each needing a transplant to survive.

- `organ-needs set`
  The medically differentiated list of needs: heart, lung, kidney, liver, pancreas.

- `no organ donors`
  The scarcity condition that removes the easy solution and forces the dilemma.

- `healthy checkup patient`
  The single nearby person whose organs could in principle supply the five.

- `utilitarian temptation`
  The pressure to treat the case as a count comparison between one death and five survivals.

- `audience test`
  The final move from abstract setup to personal judgment: ``How many would do it?''

## Derivation Steps

1. Clinical setup to count structure
   1. There are five patients.
   2. Each needs an organ transplant to survive.
   3. Their needs are medically distinct.
   4. Morally, the case is still presented as a five-person aggregate.

2. Scarcity to dilemma
   1. There are no organ donors.
   2. The five are about to die.
   3. Therefore ordinary waiting is not a neutral option inside the thought experiment.

3. Healthy patient to sacrificial proposal
   1. One healthy person is available in the next room.
   2. That one person can supply five organs.
   3. Therefore the thought experiment introduces the option of killing one to save five.

4. Count comparison to utilitarian pressure
   1. One death is set against five survivals.
   2. If each life is counted symmetrically, the arithmetic favors saving five.
   3. Therefore the case generates the implicit comparison
      \[
      5 - 1 = 4.
      \]

5. Arithmetic to audience confrontation
   1. The lecture does not stop at the calculation.
   2. Sandel asks whether anyone would actually do it.
   3. Therefore the thought experiment tests whether numerical advantage is morally sufficient.

## Notation Choices

- Use `\text{...}` for all displayed expressions; the formal content is conceptual and arithmetical, not scientific.

- Use `N_{\text{patients}}`, `N_{\text{donors}}`, and `N_{\text{healthy candidates}}` for counts.

- Use set notation only for the organ list:
  \[
  \{\text{heart},\ \text{lung},\ \text{kidney},\ \text{liver},\ \text{pancreas}\}.
  \]

- Use `\Longrightarrow` for argumentative consequence, not causal mechanism.

- Use `\Delta N_{\text{lives}}` only as an editorial shorthand for the utilitarian arithmetic; do not treat it as lecture-visible notation.

- Prefer plain phrases like `save 5 by sacrificing 1` over more loaded abstractions unless the later lecture explicitly introduces them.

- Avoid `U`, expected-value notation, or maximization over utilities unless the transcript later supports that stronger formalism.

## Uncertain Mathematics

- No equation is frame-visible; every displayed formula is editorial or transcript-backed.

- The frame directly supports only the count of five, not the organ list, not the surgeon, and not the healthy man.

- The transcript is unstable around the lines describing the healthy man and the laughter; use only the stable content that he is a healthy checkup patient in the next room, apparently sleeping.

- The comparison
  \[
  5 > 1
  \]
  is a faithful rendering of the utilitarian temptation, but it is not spoken as a literal formula.

- The line
  \[
  \Delta N_{\text{lives}} = 5 - 1 = 4
  \]
  assumes equal weighting of persons and should be presented as the argument under test, not as a settled principle of the lecture.

- Because the figure shows five identical icons, it visually flattens the five patients into symmetry; the chapter should remember that the transcript first individuates them by organ need before the arithmetic re-aggregates them.
