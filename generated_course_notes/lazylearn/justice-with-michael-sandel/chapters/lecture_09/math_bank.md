## Definitions And Objects
- \(\text{moral desert}\): worthiness of reward, honor, or position because of virtue, merit, or excellence.
- \(\text{legitimate expectations}\): entitlements generated once institutional rules and criteria are fixed.
- \(\text{academic index}\): admissions metric said to include grades and test scores; exact weighting not given.
- \(\text{corrective argument}\): race/background may be considered to correct for distorted informational meaning of scores and grades.
- \(\text{compensatory argument}\): affirmative action as compensation for past injustice or historic wrongs.
- \(\text{diversity argument}\): affirmative action justified by institutional purpose rather than by correction or compensation alone.
- \(\text{educational experience}\): first branch of diversity; diverse classmates improve education for all students.
- \(\text{wider civic mission}\): second branch of diversity; institutions train leaders who should reflect the community they serve.
- \(\text{common good}\): institutional or social good invoked by diversity and mission-based arguments.
- \(\text{individual right at stake}\): possible right to be judged by achievement or by factors within one’s control.
- \(\text{telos}\): end, goal, or purpose of a practice or thing.
- \(\text{relevant excellence}\): the merit appropriate to the thing being distributed.
- \(\text{just discrimination}\): discrimination according to the relevant excellence, not according to irrelevant traits.

## Derivation Steps
Corrective argument:
1. Admissions are supposed to track academic promise or scholarly potential.
2. Grades and test scores are used as proxies for that promise.
3. Unequal schooling can distort what those proxies mean.
4. Therefore a just reading of grades and scores may need background-sensitive correction.
5. This argument leaves academic promise as the governing aim.

Compensatory argument:
1. Past injustice can produce present racial disadvantage.
2. Admissions preference can be framed as compensation for those past wrongs.
3. Objection: a present applicant may be asked to bear costs for a wrong she did not commit.
4. Therefore the argument depends on some notion of group responsibility or responsibility across time.

Diversity argument:
1. A university has a purpose beyond ranking raw scores.
2. A diverse class may improve the education of all students.
3. A diverse class may also serve the institution’s wider civic mission.
4. Therefore race can be treated as one admissions factor in pursuit of that mission.
5. This shifts the argument from compensation to purpose.

Rawlsian rights objection:
1. Common-good reasoning does not automatically override individual rights.
2. If a student is denied admission for the sake of institutional purpose, a right may seem to be at stake.
3. Candidate right: to be judged by achievement or by factors within one’s control.
4. Rawlsian-style reply: no one morally deserves the criteria themselves.
5. Entitlement begins only after the institution defines its mission and rules.

Mission challenge:
1. If institutional mission alone justifies admissions criteria, exclusionary missions could use the same form of argument.
2. Texas segregation and Harvard anti-Jewish quotas are introduced as tests of that principle.
3. Therefore mission claims themselves must be morally assessed.
4. This prepares the turn away from neutral-rights language toward a theory of purpose and fitness.

Aristotelian allocation:
1. To ask what is just, first ask what thing is being distributed.
2. Then ask what excellence is relevant to that thing.
3. For flutes, the relevant excellence is flute playing.
4. Therefore the best flutes should go to the best flute players.
5. Allocation by wealth, birth, beauty, or chance misses the relevant excellence.

Anti-utilitarian clarification:
1. A utilitarian answer would justify allocation by best consequences.
2. Sandel explicitly rejects that as Aristotle’s reason.
3. Aristotle’s reason is that flutes are for being played well.
4. Therefore the justification comes from telos, not aggregate enjoyment.

Final synthesis:
1. Modern rights-oriented theories tend to detach justice from desert.
2. Aristotle reconnects justice with purpose, excellence, and due.
3. The affirmative-action debate can therefore be recast as a dispute over the purpose of university education.
4. That is the lecture’s final conceptual compression.
