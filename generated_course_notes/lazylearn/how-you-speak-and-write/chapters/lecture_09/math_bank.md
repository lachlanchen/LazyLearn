## Definitions And Objects
- `W`: the fictional world treated as a structured domain rather than a loose backdrop.
- `R_{\mathrm{phys}}`: the world’s physical rules, such as gravity, mortality, resurrection conditions, or skill acquisition constraints.
- `R_{\mathrm{soc}}`: the world’s societal rules, such as government, power, punishments, beliefs, and values.
- `book-world`: the internally coherent domain that the reader learns while reading.
- `outside world`: ordinary reality as contrasted with the fictional domain; in the accepted frame this is represented by the background cluster of figures.
- `timeline`: an ordered account of past events that explains the present form of the world.
- `macro questionnaire`: the high-level state description of the world, centered on law, power, belief, and value.
- `micro questionnaire`: the lived texture of the world, centered on weather, work, food, ecology, technology, and ordinary routines.
- `conflict`: the narrative pressure that emerges once characters are released into the world’s constraints.
- `worldbuilding`: the construction of a consistent rule-governed domain from which stories can later grow.

## Derivation Steps
Rule coherence from the opening examples
1. Start with several famous fictional cases.
2. Notice that each case encodes a nontrivial rule rather than a random marvel.
3. Infer that memorable worlds are governed by stable internal constraints.
4. Conclude that consistency is what makes the world believable and readable.

Real law versus page law
1. Begin from a law we take as stable in ordinary life: gravity.
2. Observe that the book as physical object still obeys that law in our world.
3. Shift domains from bookshelf reality to the pages of the fiction.
4. Note that within the fictional domain the law can be altered or suspended.
5. Conclude that worldbuilding specifies which laws survive and which laws are rewritten.

From world structure to reader understanding
1. Build more than a single gimmick or exception.
2. Add maps, lineages, languages, cultures, and large-scale setting structure.
3. Make the rule system consistent across the domain.
4. Let the reader accumulate internal knowledge of that domain.
5. Reach the point where the reader can track the book-world as well as the inhabitants do.

From marks on a page to changed perspective
1. Put marks on a page.
2. Let light from the page reach the eyes.
3. Send signals to the brain.
4. Decode those signals as narrative structure.
5. Recruit emotion, thought, and imaginative participation.
6. Produce immersion in the fictional world.
7. Allow that immersion to alter the reader’s perspective on the real world.

Practical worldbuilding method
1. Reconstruct how the world came to be.
2. Order those prior events into a timeline.
3. Ask what rules are in force.
4. Ask who governs, who has power, and what people believe.
5. Ask what the society values.
6. Move from structure to daily life: weather, school, food, play, age, ecology, and technology.
7. Keep filling in the world until it is internally inhabitable.

From world to story
1. Know the world as well as you want the reader to know it.
2. Place characters inside that world.
3. Ask how the world shapes those characters.
4. Ask what conflicts are likely under those conditions.
5. Let conflict generate the story.

## Notation Choices
- Use `W` for the world as a whole only in editorial compressions, not as lecture-visible notation.
- Use `R_{\mathrm{phys}}` and `R_{\mathrm{soc}}` for physical and societal rule-sets.
- Use `\Rightarrow` for causal or generative claims: consistency yields believability; world plus characters yields story.
- Use `\to` for ordered progression or process: page marks to signals to decoding; past events to timeline to present world.
- Use `\iff` only for sharply rule-like biconditionals such as the Quidditch end condition.
- Use `\neq` only for the gravity contrast, and label it clearly as editorial shorthand rather than visible lecture notation.
- Use `\gtrsim` for the “as well as or even better” comparison, with explicit caution that it is an interpretive compression, not spoken symbolic notation.
- Keep all narrative objects in upright text inside equations: `\text{...}`.
- Treat the macro and micro questionnaires as tuples or state-vectors, not as literal linear-algebra objects.
- Do not elevate the faint mark in the accepted frame into notation.
