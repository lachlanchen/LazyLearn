## Definitions And Objects

- `3,2,1`: the lecture’s core communication framework.
- `Framework`: the fallback structure that replaces rambling under pressure.
- `F_{321}(T)`: editorial symbol for applying the `3,2,1` scaffold to topic `T`.
- `T`: arbitrary speaking topic, e.g. avocado or travel.
- `1 thing`: the single easiest salient point about a topic; Zhang uses this as the quickest entry point.
- `2 types`: two categories or two ways of treating the topic; the lecture also informally switches this to “two ways.”
- `3 steps`: a procedural decomposition of the topic into three ordered actions or stages.
- `panic search`: the uncontrolled mental branching that occurs when one is asked a question without preparation.
- `fallback`: the structure the mind defaults to when speaking under pressure.
- `S_1,S_2,S_3,S_4`: partially visible stage labels on the auxiliary right-hand board; evidence for a broader staged framework, but not secure enough for exact semantic assignment.
- `Visibility`: heading of a broader board hierarchy in the promo insert, indicating that `3,2,1` is shown as one framework among others.

## Derivation Steps

1. Opening causal diagnosis
   1. Start with the situation: a question arrives and the speaker is unprepared.
   2. The brain “hits the panic button.”
   3. Thought branches proliferate instead of settling into one line.
   4. Verbalization begins before structure has formed.
   5. Result: rambling replaces clear points.

2. From rambling to frameworks
   1. Ask what we fall back on when unprepared.
   2. First answer: rambling.
   3. Introduce frameworks as the alternative fallback.
   4. Conclude that the main technical move is to replace an uncontrolled fallback with a controlled one.

3. Definition of the `3,2,1` scaffold
   1. Introduce the mnemonic `3,2,1`.
   2. Expand it as “three steps, two types, one thing.”
   3. Repeat the expansion to fix it rhythmically in memory.
   4. Use it not as a theorem, but as an answer-generating template.

4. Avocado example: one-thing entry
   1. Choose a random topic: avocado.
   2. Enter through the easiest slot, “one thing.”
   3. Give one salient claim about avocado.
   4. Use that as an anchor instead of searching the whole topic space.
   5. Conclude that the framework gives the mind something to lean on.

5. Avocado example: contrast with unstructured branching
   1. Enumerate possible uncontrolled directions: color, timing, ripeness, storage, recipes.
   2. Show how these possibilities proliferate without order.
   3. Observe that this branching produces blankness and tongue-twisting.
   4. Reassert that the framework suppresses this uncontrolled spread.

6. Avocado example: two-types move
   1. Shift from “one thing” to “two types.”
   2. Relax the label from “types” to “ways,” showing practical flexibility.
   3. Produce two ways to have an avocado.
   4. Conclude that the same framework can be instantiated in multiple modes.

7. Avocado example: three-steps move
   1. Shift from “two types” to “three steps.”
   2. Give a sequential preparation procedure.
   3. Use the three-step decomposition to show procedural clarity.
   4. Conclude that the framework handles both classification and process.

8. Live student drill
   1. Move from demonstration to student exercise.
   2. Impose time pressure: formulate an answer in three seconds.
   3. Give the topic “travel.”
   4. Student produces one thing, then two types, then three steps.
   5. Use successful performance as evidence that the scaffold is transferable.

9. Final interpretive step
   1. Compare framework-backed speaking with unprepared speaking.
   2. Note the emotional shift: from fear to excitement.
   3. Note the bodily shift: leaning into the microphone, moving forward.
   4. Conclude that framework use changes not just content organization, but readiness and confidence.

## Notation Choices

- Use `F_{321}` for the lecture’s main framework.
- Use topic argument notation `F_{321}(T)` only as editorial shorthand; the lecturer never writes it explicitly.
- Keep the scaffold in the lecture’s own order when defining it:
  \[
  3\ \text{steps},\ 2\ \text{types},\ 1\ \text{thing}.
  \]
- When discussing practical entry, allow editorial reordering into the set of admissible entry points:
  one thing, two types/ways, three steps.
- Use `\Rightarrow` for causal or practical implication, not for formal proof.
- Use `\leadsto` for concrete instantiation on examples like avocado and travel.
- Use `S_1,S_2,S_3,S_4` for the partially visible right-board stages, but do not assign precise names without stronger evidence.
- Keep “types” and “ways” distinct in the notes:
  the lecture defines the slot as “types,” then locally relaxes it to “ways.”
- Treat `\text{Visibility}` and `2)\ (3,2,1)` as board-hierarchy labels, not as mathematical operators.
- Keep all notation lightweight and explicitly editorial where it goes beyond what is directly visible on the boards.
