## Definitions And Objects
- `\text{dignity}`: the status attaching to rational beings and grounding respect.
- `\text{reason}`: the rational capacity shared by all persons; later specified as practical reason.
- `\text{autonomy}`: self-legislation; the will determined by a law one gives oneself.
- `\text{heteronomy}`: the will determined by inclination, desire, appetite, or externally given ends.
- `\text{necessity}`: causal determination by nature; the opposite pole from freedom in the lecture.
- `\text{good will}`: a will good in itself, not because of what it accomplishes.
- `\text{duty}`: the motive that alone confers moral worth on action.
- `\text{inclination}`: desires, wants, preferences, impulses, sympathy, self-interest, and similar contingent motives.
- `\text{imperative}`: a command of reason; an “ought.”
- `\text{hypothetical imperative}`: an instrumental command, conditionally ordered to some further end.
- `\text{categorical imperative}`: an unconditional command, independent of any further purpose.
- `\text{maxim}`: the rule or principle that explains why one is acting.
- `\text{universal law}`: the form a maxim must be able to take if it is to be morally valid.
- `\text{humanity}`: rational nature in persons, whether in oneself or others.
- `\text{end in itself}`: that which has absolute value and is not open to arbitrary use merely as a means.
- `\text{moral worth}`: the value of an action insofar as it is done from duty.

## Derivation Steps
Autonomy, freedom, and dignity
1. We are not only creatures of pleasure and pain.
2. We are rational beings capable of choosing.
3. If the will is ruled by appetite, desire, or impulse, it is ruled by natural necessity.
4. What is ruled by natural necessity is not free in Kant’s demanding sense.
5. Therefore freedom must mean autonomy, not desire-satisfaction.
6. If autonomy is self-legislation, then persons are more than instruments to externally given ends.
7. Hence rational agency grounds dignity.

From freedom to motive
1. If freedom is autonomy, then free action is not merely choosing effective means.
2. Free action is choosing or endorsing an end for its own sake.
3. Therefore morality cannot be grounded in consequences alone.
4. The moral question becomes: from what motive is the act done?
5. Kant’s answer is that only duty confers moral worth.

Shopkeeper structure
1. The shopkeeper gives correct change.
2. The act outwardly conforms to what duty requires.
3. His motive is business reputation and self-interest.
4. Therefore the act is right in outcome but lacks moral worth.
5. This isolates the distinction between conformity with duty and action from duty.

Suicide example as motive-isolation
1. Most people have many motives for continuing to live.
2. To isolate the relevant motive, Kant imagines a miserable person with no inclination toward life.
3. The person nonetheless refrains from suicide from duty.
4. Therefore what matters for moral worth is not inclination but duty.

Mixed motives and reverence
1. A student worries that once we aim to be moral, motive becomes self-defeating.
2. Sandel answers that self-interested incentive would indeed defeat moral worth.
3. Kant therefore invokes reverence for the moral law rather than self-interest.
4. Supporting feelings may accompany the act.
5. But only duty or reverence for the law grounds moral worth.

Why self-legislation is not subjectivism
1. Autonomy means acting according to a law I give myself.
2. This seems to threaten a plurality of private moral laws.
3. Sandel answers that the legislating reason is shared practical reason.
4. Shared practical reason is not idiosyncratic or biographical.
5. Therefore autonomy yields one universal moral law rather than many private consciences.

Imperative distinction
1. Reason can command in more than one way.
2. If reason says “If you want \(X\), do \(Y\),” the command is conditional on a further end.
3. Such a command is hypothetical and instrumental.
4. If an action is good in itself and necessary for a will in accord with reason, the command is unconditional.
5. Such a command is categorical.
6. Therefore autonomous morality requires categorical, not hypothetical, imperative structure.

Universal-law test
1. Begin with a maxim explaining the proposed action.
2. Universalize that maxim.
3. Ask whether the universalized maxim undermines the very practice it presupposes.
4. In the false-promise case, universalized deception destroys the institution of promising.
5. Therefore the maxim fails the test and is morally impermissible.
6. Sandel insists this is a test of a maxim, not a direct appeal to consequences.

Humanity formula
1. A categorical imperative cannot rest on merely private interests or contingent ends.
2. Therefore its ground must lie in something with absolute value.
3. Rational beings have that absolute value as ends in themselves.
4. Hence we must treat humanity, in self and others, never merely as a means.
5. False promise, murder, and suicide violate this by subordinating rational being to some further purpose.

Use versus mere use
1. Everyday action regularly involves using others as means in ordinary cooperation.
2. Kant’s prohibition is not against all use.
3. The prohibition is against using persons merely as means.
4. Therefore ordinary market and social interaction are not automatically wrong.
5. What matters is whether dignity and rational agency are respected.

## Notation Choices
- Use `\text{...}` for all lecture-native conceptual equations; the lecture does not introduce symbolic variables beyond schematic placeholders like \(X\) and \(Y\).
- Use `\Longrightarrow` for one-way argumentative dependence and `\not\Longrightarrow` when the lecture denies moral sufficiency.
- Use `\leftrightarrow` only for paired contrasts, not for strict equivalence claims unless the lecture is explicitly framing a dualism.
- Keep `X` and `Y` only in the hypothetical-imperative schema:
  \[
  \text{If you want } X,\ \text{do } Y
  \]
- Treat `\text{maxim}` as the lecture’s operative object for testing universalizability; do not introduce extra symbols like \(M\) unless later drafting needs compression.
- Prefer `\text{humanity}` over `\text{personhood}` because Sandel quotes Kant’s humanity formula directly.
- Prefer `\text{moral worth}` over `\text{moral value}` to match the lecture’s repeated phrase.
- Prefer `\text{good will}` over symbolic abbreviations.
- Use `\text{means}` and `\text{end}` in ordinary text form; no special operator notation is warranted.
- Keep the visible slide contrast in compact reconstructed form rather than reproducing the long quotation.
