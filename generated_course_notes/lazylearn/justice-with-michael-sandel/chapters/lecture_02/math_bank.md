## Definitions And Objects
- \(U\): utility; the lecture’s master quantity.
- \(H\): happiness or pleasure.
- \(S\): suffering or pain.
- \(u_i\): individual contribution to aggregate utility; editorial shorthand for Bentham’s “community” as sum of individuals.
- \(a\): act, policy, or legal rule under comparison.
- \(\text{Benefits}(a)\), \(\text{Costs}(a)\), \(\text{Net}(a)\): cost-benefit ledger entries.
- \(V(\text{life})\): dollar proxy assigned to human life inside a cost-benefit analysis; philosophically contested by the lecture.
- \(V(x)\): willingness-to-accept dollar amount in Thorndike-style survey items.
- \(\operatorname{Qty}(p)\): quantity of pleasure for activity \(p\), meant in Bentham’s intensity-duration sense.
- \(p_1 \sim p_2\): equal in value when equal in quantity of pleasure; only schematic here.
- \(\text{Higher}(p_1,p_2)\): Mill’s qualitative ranking of pleasures.
- “Competent judges” / “those who have experienced both”: Mill’s key comparison class.
- \(\text{Preferred}\): what people like more in the moment.
- \(\text{Higher}\): what people judge nobler / worthier / qualitatively better.
- “Commensurability”: assumption that unlike goods can be translated into one common scale.
- “Justice and rights”: Mill’s specially weighty class of moral requirements within utility.

## Derivation Steps
Bentham’s maximization principle
1. Start from the claim that human beings are governed by pleasure and pain.
2. Conclude that any moral system must take those quantities into account.
3. Bentham’s move is to take them into account by maximizing their balance.
4. Translate that into “greatest good for the greatest number.”
5. Extend the same logic from personal conduct to law and legislation.

Policy calculus
1. Treat a community as the sum of the individuals who compose it.
2. For each policy, list benefits and costs.
3. Add all benefits across persons and institutions.
4. Subtract all costs.
5. Choose the policy with the highest net balance.

Czech smoking study
1. Put smoking-related health expenditures on the cost side.
2. Put cigarette tax revenues on the benefit side.
3. Add pension savings from earlier death.
4. Add housing and health-care savings from earlier death.
5. Compute a net fiscal gain of \(\$147\) million.
6. Expose the moral problem: early death now appears as a public benefit.

Ford Pinto memo
1. Estimate the safety fix at \(\$11\) per vehicle.
2. Multiply across \(12.5\) million cars and trucks.
3. Get a total safety cost of about \(\$137\) million as narrated.
4. Price expected deaths, injuries, and destroyed vehicles.
5. Get a reported benefit total of about \(\$49.5\) million.
6. Compare benefit to cost.
7. Do not install the device because the benefit total is lower.

Rights objection
1. Accept the utilitarian rule of aggregation for the moment.
2. Ask whether many people’s pleasure can outweigh one person’s extreme suffering.
3. Use the Roman arena case to force the issue.
4. Notice that pure aggregation threatens individual and minority rights.
5. Extract the first objection: utility may not respect what is owed to persons.

Commensurability objection
1. Assume all goods can be translated into one common measure.
2. Use Thorndike’s survey to assign dollar values to unlike experiences.
3. Rank those experiences on one scale.
4. Observe that the ranking itself looks preposterous or distorting.
5. Extract the second objection: some values may not survive translation into one monetary metric.

Bentham on quantity, not quality
1. Refuse intrinsic rankings among pleasures.
2. Count only intensity and duration.
3. If quantitative yield is equal, treat the activities as equal in value.
4. Use “pushpin is as good as poetry” as the compact slogan.

Mill’s competent-judge repair
1. Keep the utilitarian premise that actual desires are the only admissible evidence.
2. Do not appeal to an external moral standard.
3. Compare two pleasures only among those who have experienced both.
4. If all or almost all such judges prefer one, call it the more desirable pleasure.
5. Use this to distinguish higher from lower pleasures within utilitarian terms.

Mill on rights
1. Keep utility as the ultimate moral standard.
2. Treat justice and rights as the most sacred and binding part of morality.
3. Ground that priority in higher social utility.
4. Justify the priority by long-run human interests.
5. Leave open whether this still counts as purely utilitarian.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(U\) for aggregate utility throughout.
- Use \(H\) and \(S\) rather than separate symbols for pleasure vs. happiness and pain vs. suffering; the lecture treats those pairs as near-equivalents.
- Use \(u_i\) only when explicit person-by-person aggregation is needed.
- Use \(a\) for act, policy, or legal rule; do not proliferate separate symbols unless the comparison requires it.
- Use roman-text ledger labels \(\text{Benefits}\), \(\text{Costs}\), \(\text{Net}\) instead of bare \(B,C,N\) where readability matters.
- Use \(V(x)\) only for assigned dollar valuations or willingness-to-accept amounts, not for objective moral worth.
- Keep \(\arg\max\) as editorial shorthand, not as claimed lecture notation.
- Keep Mill’s criterion mostly verbal; any formalization should remain schematic.
- If a poll table is used, keep two distinct columns: \(\text{Preferred}\) and \(\text{Higher}\).
- Keep dollar units explicit inside equations: \(\$147\,\text{million}\), \(\$49.5\,\text{million}\), \(\$4{,}500\).
- Do not introduce probability notation, expected value notation, or welfare-function machinery not spoken in the lecture.

## Uncertain Mathematics
- No item here is frame-backed; there are no validated visual equations or diagrams for this lecture.
- \(U_{\text{community}} = \sum_i u_i\) is editorial shorthand for Bentham’s “community” as sum of individuals, not a visible lecture formula.
- The Ford Pinto injury coefficient is missing in the transcript; keep the term symbolic or tabular, and preserve only the reported bottom-line total.
- The Pinto multiplication is approximate: \(11 \times 12.5\text{ million} = 137.5\text{ million}\), while Sandel states \(\$137\) million.
- The cell-phone example has a death-count instability: the lecture first says about \(2000\) deaths annually, later \(2300\).
- Thorndike’s ranking should be used as evidence of strain on commensurability, not as proof that all values are measurable.
- Bentham’s “pushpin is as good as poetry” should remain a schematic slogan, not a fully developed equivalence law.
- Mill’s “all or almost all” clause and his exclusion of external moral pressure matter; do not compress them into a too-clean symbolic rule without a prose qualifier.
- Mill’s claim that justice and rights stand higher in social utility is ordinal and conceptual, not a measured quantitative inequality.
- The out-of-order transcript splice around the early poll should not control the formal presentation; use the later Shakespeare / Fear Factor / Simpsons sequence as the stable evidence for the preference-vs-higher distinction.
