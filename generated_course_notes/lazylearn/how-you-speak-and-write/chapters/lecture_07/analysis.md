# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
The lecture opens not with abstract advice, but with place and process. We begin in Follett's den, then immediately undercut the romance of place by the remark that one can write anywhere. That matters because it prevents the room from becoming the lesson; the lesson is method, and the room is only the entry point.

From there the interviewer pivots to the three-screen setup, and the lecture becomes procedural. Follett explains what each screen is doing: a first draft on one side, external notes on another, and the next draft in progress at the center. This is the first real structural move of the lecture, because writing is recast as a controlled information flow between draft, criticism, and rewrite.

The next movement goes from local example to principle. A specific reader comment about not being drawn in quickly enough becomes a diagnosis of a ponderous opening and then a reminder of Follett's pacing rule about a plot twist every three to four pages. The lecture's rhythm is important here: comment, inference, general rule. That sequence should survive in the notes.

The interviewer then tests Follett's stance toward criticism. Would he dismiss the comment because of his status, or learn from it? That tension matters because it clarifies the epistemology of revision: feedback is not an insult to authorship but an instrument for seeing what the draft is actually doing.

From there the lecture widens into the large-scale process. Follett describes not line-editing the first draft but retyping the entire second draft from scratch, using the old material and the comments as guides. This leads directly into the time-budget arithmetic of the talk: planning takes a year, first draft a year, second draft a year. That temporal decomposition is one of the lecture's clearest mathematical spines.

The next pivot is motivational. The interviewer challenges the necessity of this labor, and Follett answers by asserting that the second draft from scratch produces a superior book. The lecture then turns inward, from workflow to psychology, by asking about angst and writer's block.

The final major movement is the crisis beat. Follett denies a full existential crisis, but names a recurring moment deep into every book when, after hundreds of pages, doubt becomes severe. The lecture resolves that crisis pragmatically: press on, set the thought aside, and trust revision. It ends not with struggle but with affirmation. Follett insists that he loves the entire process. That closing emotional register should remain in the notes, because it reframes rigor as devotion rather than punishment.

## Section Outline
1. **The Room, the Myth of Place, and the Turn to Process.** Open with the den and the immediate correction that writing can happen anywhere, so that the chapter begins by separating atmosphere from method. A standalone `Question & Answer` should appear here: `Does a writer need the ideal room, or only a working process?`

2. **Three Screens and the Flow from Draft to Feedback to Rewrite.** Build the first technical section around the screen layout and what each screen represents: first draft, reader comments, and second draft in progress. A standalone `Question & Answer` should appear here: `What information belongs in front of the writer while revising?`

3. **Reader Comments as Diagnostics of Pace.** Use the example comment about not being drawn in quickly enough to derive the diagnosis of a ponderous opening and the pacing rule about a twist every three to four pages. A standalone `Question & Answer` should appear here: `How does a comment become a structural diagnosis?`

4. **Revision as Full Recomposition, Not Surface Editing.** Center this section on Follett's method of rewriting the second draft from scratch rather than marking up the first draft. A standalone `Question & Answer` should appear here: `Why rewrite the whole book instead of simply editing the first draft?`

5. **The Arithmetic of Long-Form Writing.** Turn the process into time structure: one year planning, one year first draft, one year second draft. This section should make the lecture's hidden quantitative logic explicit without turning it into artificial algebra.

6. **Doubt in the Middle and the Discipline of Pressing On.** Use the later part of the interview to capture the recurring moment of severe doubt after several hundred pages, and Follett's rule for surviving it by continuing and trusting revision. A standalone `Question & Answer` should appear here: `What should we do when a book suddenly seems unreadable to its own author?`

7. **Standards, Absorption, and Love of the Work.** Conclude with the relation between high standards, repeated rewriting, and Follett's insistence that he is absorbed by the whole process. End on the lecture's own emotional resolution rather than on generic productivity advice.
