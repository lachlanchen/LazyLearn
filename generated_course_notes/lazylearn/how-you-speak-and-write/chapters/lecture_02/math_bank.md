## Definitions And Objects
- `winning story idea`: the single candidate idea that has enough passion and enough weight to carry a full novel.
- `marathon of the middle`: the center stretch between opener and ending where drama, tension, conflict, action, and setups must not fade.
- `outliner`: a writer who wants the story mapped in advance.
- `pantser`: a writer who discovers the story by writing.
- `hybrid`: a writer who uses some outline security while preserving discovery.
- `classic story structure`: Jenkins’s quoted Koontz sequence of trouble, worsening trouble, apparent hopelessness, and earned victory.
- `protagonist` / `lead` / `hero`: the central character, expected to undergo a character arc.
- `character arc`: the protagonist’s change, ideally toward a better self by the end.
- `antagonist`: the opposing force; should be formidable and believe in his own reasons.
- `orbital characters`: important secondary characters who must be more than caricatures.
- `outward problem`: the visible external problem facing the lead.
- `inward turmoil` / `inner weakness`: the internal struggle that gives the lead depth.
- `point-of-view character`: the character whose perspective functions as the story’s camera.
- `first person`, `second person`, `third person limited`, `omniscient viewpoint`: the viewpoint modes explicitly named in the lecture.
- `in medias res`: beginning in the midst of things.
- `surprise opener`, `dramatic statement`, `philosophical opener`, `poetic opener`: the four opening-line classes Jenkins lists.
- `showing`: presenting evidence from which the reader infers a fact or state.
- `telling`: directly reporting the fact or state.
- `bleakest moment`: Angela Hunt’s term for the lowest point where repair seems impossible.
- `climax`: the peak emotional and conflict resolution point.
- `ending`: the post-climax resolution that leaves the reader satisfied.
- `bonus step`: separating writing and editing, with a referenced but not transcribed 21-part checklist.

## Derivation Steps
Winning-idea filter
1. Start from many possible story ideas.
2. Ask which one draws the writer back to the keyboard.
3. Test that idea against the middle stretch, not just the opening spark.
4. Keep the idea whose passion can survive discouragement and repetition.

Outliner/pantser to structure
1. Distinguish between planning-first and discovery-first methods.
2. Refuse to rank them morally.
3. Note that many writers are hybrids.
4. Conclude that some usable structure is still needed to avoid collapse after the opening pages.

Koontz four-step backbone
1. Put the protagonist into terrible trouble early.
2. Make every attempt at escape worsen the situation.
3. Push the situation until it appears hopeless.
4. Resolve the story through the protagonist’s developed strength and insight.

Point-of-view information boundary
1. Choose the scene’s perspective character.
2. Restrict narration to what that character can perceive or think.
3. Avoid hopping across multiple minds within the same scene.
4. Prefer the character with the most at stake in the scene.

Show-versus-tell conversion
1. Identify the abstract report: tall, angry, cold, and so on.
2. Replace the report with observable cues.
3. Let the reader infer the state from those cues.
4. Remove the redundant direct statement if the inference is now clear.

Conflict escalation
1. Begin with a compelling opener and real trouble.
2. Make each attempted solution intensify the problem.
3. Strip away ease, safety, and convenience.
4. Use rising conflict to sustain reader attention.

Bleakest moment to ending
1. Push the hero to the nadir where even repair seems impossible.
2. Do not rescue the hero early with an easy escape or miracle.
3. Force action through everything learned along the way.
4. Let the climax resolve the major setups.
5. Add a distinct ending that satisfies after the climax.

Drafting versus editing
1. Draft with the inner critic muted.
2. Get the story down without stopping for perfection.
3. Return later in a separate pass for revision.
4. Fine-tune word by word only after the drafting mode is finished.

## Notation Choices
- Use minimal notation; this lecture is procedural, not symbolic.
- Let \(I\) denote the chosen story idea when a compact symbol is useful.
- Let \(H\) denote the hero / protagonist.
- Let \(A\) denote the antagonist.
- Let \(s\) denote a scene.
- Let \(\mathrm{POV}(s)\) denote the point-of-view character for scene \(s\).
- Let \(\mathrm{Info}(s)\) denote the information available through that viewpoint.
- If a compact escalation notation is needed, let \(T_0 \to T_1 \to T_2 \to T_3\) denote the Koontz progression from initial trouble to earned victory.
- Use \(\to\) for narrative progression.
- Use \(\Rightarrow\) for practical or reader-response consequence.
- Use \(\neq\) only for hard distinctions such as climax versus ending.
- Default prose term: `protagonist`; keep `hero` when echoing Jenkins’s own wording.
- Default prose term: `third person limited`; reserve `omniscient viewpoint` for the mode he warns against.
- Do not introduce pseudo-physics objects, operators, or extremization notation unless the final chapter explicitly labels them as editorial shorthand.
