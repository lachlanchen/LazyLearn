# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- We begin exactly where Sandel begins: not with theory, but with a vivid trolley story and an immediate poll. The lecture first establishes raw moral intuition under pressure, then pivots to the reasons people give for their votes.
- Sandel then changes one structural feature at a time while preserving the basic one-versus-many tension: driver becomes onlooker, steering becomes pushing, triage becomes organ harvesting. The lecture’s first real engine is comparative case variation.
- Once the class’s responses become unstable, Sandel explicitly steps back and names two rival moral logics emerging from the discussion: consequentialist reasoning and categorical reasoning. This recap is a major hinge and should read as a deliberate pause for conceptual sorting.
- He then broadens from the opening puzzles to the aim of the course itself, naming Bentham and Kant, connecting classic texts to live controversies, and warning that philosophy unsettles inherited certainties. This is a motivational transition, not filler.
- After the internal break, Sandel recaps both method and substance: we move from judgments in cases to principles, then back again under pressure from new cases. Only then does he introduce Bentham in a more systematic way and define utilitarianism.
- The Dudley and Stevens case becomes the final stress test. Sandel first lets the survival arithmetic and necessity defense do their work, then forces the class to face objections about murder, fair procedure, and consent, ending with three precise philosophical questions that launch the next stage of the course.
- The chapter voice should preserve this guided movement: we begin with a story, we vary the case, we step back, we name the principle, we test it again, and we end with sharper unanswered questions.

## Section Outline
1. Opening the Course Through the Trolley Problem. We introduce justice through the runaway trolley and keep the poll structure, because Sandel wants the chapter to start from lived judgment before abstract doctrine.
2. Trolley, Fat Man, and the Breakdown of a Simple Maxim. This section should contain a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Question & Answer: Why does turning the trolley seem permissible while pushing the fat man does not?`
3. Doctor, Triage, and Transplant. We preserve the medical variants in sequence because they extend the same numerical pattern while revealing that many listeners resist outcome-counting once the act itself looks like murder.
4. Two Moral Logics Emerge. This is the first conceptual consolidation: consequentialist reasoning is introduced from the class’s own responses, then categorical reasoning is introduced as the rival view that some acts are wrong regardless of results.
5. Why Philosophy Matters, and Why It Disturbs. We keep Sandel’s course-setting material as a real section, because it explains why these examples are not warm-up puzzles but a way of estranging the familiar, raising the stakes personally and politically, and confronting skepticism.
6. Bentham, Utility, and the Return to Method. After the break, we restate the lecture’s method of moving between cases and principles, then introduce utilitarianism in Bentham’s own basic terms: utility as pleasure over pain, happiness over suffering, and the greatest good for the greatest number.
7. Dudley and Stevens: Necessity, Lottery, and Consent. This section should contain a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Question & Answer: Would lottery or consent make the killing of Parker morally permissible?` It should end by preserving Sandel’s three launch questions about rights, fair procedure, and the moral force of consent.
