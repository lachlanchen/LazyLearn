## Definitions And Objects

- `N_{\text{victims}}`
  The numerical magnitude of the Holocaust introduced by Sandel as ``the genocide of six million.''

- `\text{human dignity}`
  The post-war moral language Germany uses to come to terms with the Holocaust.

- `\text{collective crime}`
  A crime attributed not merely to isolated individuals but to an entire society.

- `\text{collective responsibility}`
  The inherited moral burden claimed by later generations in response to collective crime.

- `\text{individual guilt}`
  Personal culpability for acts one oneself committed or directly endorsed.

- `\text{Kantian responsibility}`
  Responsibility restricted to acts freely chosen by the agent.

- `\text{freely chosen act}`
  The basic unit of moral attribution in Sandel's summary of Kant.

- `\text{historical burden}`
  The inherited moral weight carried by a later generation because of a nation's past.

- `\text{guilt}`, `\text{shame}`, `\text{inheritance}`
  The psychological and moral vocabulary the interviewee thinks Kant likely does not handle well.

- `\text{instrument}`
  The Kantian concern that one generation may be used merely as a means for another if responsibility is transmitted across generations.

- `\text{neutral politics}`
  The modern aspiration to avoid judgments about virtue and the good life in politics.

- `\text{good life}`
  Aristotle's positive end of politics in the lecture's closing turn.

## Derivation Steps

1. Historical burden to numerical scale
   1. Germany is described through memorials, ruins, and historical traces.
   2. Sandel names ``the genocide of six million.''
   3. Therefore the burden is framed as both historical and collective, not private and local.

2. Human dignity as post-war response
   1. Germany's post-war generations insist on human dignity.
   2. This insistence is presented as a way of coming to terms with the Holocaust.
   3. Therefore dignity is introduced as a moral response to historical catastrophe.

3. Collective crime to collective responsibility
   1. The interviewee says the crimes were not committed only by individuals.
   2. She says they were committed by an entire society.
   3. Therefore:
      \[
      \text{collective crime}
      \Longrightarrow
      \text{collective responsibility}
      \]

4. Private guilt is denied as the basis
   1. It does not matter whether she personally committed the crime.
   2. It does not matter whether her grandparents personally committed crimes.
   3. Therefore inherited responsibility is not being grounded in individual guilt.

5. Kantian limit
   1. For Kant, responsibility attaches only to acts freely chosen.
   2. A later generation did not freely choose the crimes of earlier generations.
   3. Therefore Kant appears unable to ground collective historical responsibility.

6. Identity-based responsibility is tested
   1. Sandel asks whether identity shaped by nation, culture, and history could matter morally.
   2. The interviewee says Kant probably could not make sense of inherited guilt or shame.
   3. Therefore the lecture sharpens the contrast between embedded identity and freely chosen action.

7. Instrument problem
   1. Suppose a later generation takes responsibility for an earlier generation.
   2. Then the later generation may be assigned a burden not of its own choosing.
   3. The interviewee suggests this would make the later generation an instrument of the earlier.
   4. Therefore Kant may have a principled objection, not only a psychological blind spot.

8. Politics widens beyond responsibility
   1. Sandel then asks whether politics can be neutral on moral and spiritual questions.
   2. He turns to Aristotle as an alternative.
   3. Aristotle is introduced by denying that politics is merely about GDP.
   4. Therefore the lecture shifts from responsibility to the good life as the end of politics.

## Notation Choices

- Use `\text{...}` for all displayed expressions. The lecture's formal content is conceptual, historical, and normative rather than algebraic.

- Use `N_{\text{victims}}` for the single explicit numerical datum:
  \[
  N_{\text{victims}} = 6 \times 10^6.
  \]

- Use `\Longrightarrow` for argumentative consequence, not causal law.

- Use `\neq` for distinctions the lecture treats as structurally important:
  \[
  \text{individual guilt} \neq \text{collective responsibility}, \qquad
  \text{politics} \neq \text{maximizing GDP}.
  \]

- Use `\stackrel{?}{=}` or `\stackrel{?}{\Longrightarrow}` only for contested claims the lecture raises but does not settle:
  \[
  \text{collective historical burden} \stackrel{?}{=} \text{moral responsibility}.
  \]

- Avoid introducing symbols for utility, rights, virtue, or agency unless the chapter later needs them. The lecture itself does not formalize at that level.

- Avoid formulas that look stronger than the transcript. Prefer compact verbal equations over abstract logical notation.
