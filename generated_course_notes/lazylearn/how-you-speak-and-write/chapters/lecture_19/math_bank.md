## Definitions And Objects
- `tenor`: the thing actually being described in a metaphor.
- `vehicle`: the thing brought in to carry the description elsewhere.
- `correspondence`: the operative relation between tenor and vehicle; in this lecture, often behavioral rather than merely visual.
- `mimesis`: recognizable rendering of what is already known.
- `poiesis`: process-region between fixed names; the threshold where change is happening.
- `estrangement`: making the familiar perceptible again by displacement, re-description, or altered correspondence.
- `recognition`: naming the tendencies already present in a draft before correcting them.
- `dogma`: inherited workshop rule that cannot survive repeated “why?” questioning.
- `sentence`: not just grammatical unit, but mind/consciousness passing through syntax.
- `syntax`: the shaping mechanism that lets perception enter and remain in the reader.
- `haunting`: Vuong’s preferred reader-effect over mere hooking; the work stays with the reader.
- `pattern`: the linear expectation structure shared by prose, music, film, skate videos, and performance.
- `assignment`: external obligation such as plot, reportorial duty, or explanatory burden.
- `laboratory`: a form-space where language can be tried without immediate service to plot or information.
- `synchronic reading`: reading a work inside its immediate time-slot, season, or contemporary institutional frame.
- `diachronic reading`: reading a work against the longer temporal accumulation of other works and inherited context.
- `house style`: institutional standardization of prose for brand, efficacy, and recognizability.
- `original matrix`: the student’s initial structure of awe/novelty before institutional flattening.
- `use`: late-Wittgenstein sense in which word meaning is produced by lived deployment, not only by dictionary definition.

## Derivation Steps
1. `Metaphor -> re-seeing`
2. Identify the tenor.
3. Choose a vehicle from sustained observation, not from stock resemblance.
4. Build a correspondence between tenor and vehicle.
5. If the correspondence changes how the scene is perceived, the sentence exits mimesis.
6. Babel’s sunset works because the added image carries war consciousness into the scene.

1. `Babel -> altered rate of perception`
2. Start with a standard sunset description.
3. Add “as if beheaded.”
4. The clause adds connotation, not just decoration.
5. The added violence changes the felt speed and motion of the sunset.
6. Therefore metaphor modifies perception dynamically, not only semantically.

1. `Workshop correction -> workshop recognition`
2. Excess feedback treats correction as progress.
3. Over-correction can move the draft away from its own center.
4. Recognition first names recurring tendencies in diction, lineation, tense, syntax, image choice.
5. Those tendencies reveal the writer to themself.
6. Revision becomes guided by pattern, not by imported rule.

1. `Sentence as filtered consciousness`
2. A sentence is not only information transfer.
3. Each writer’s syntax filters perception differently.
4. Therefore recurring formal habits are traces of consciousness.
5. Reading those habits is part of reading the writer.

1. `Historical taming of the sentence`
2. Oratorical prose valued delay, subordination, and buildup.
3. Post-Civil-War newspaper prose demanded clarity, brevity, and reliability.
4. The novel’s rise as a serious public form overlapped with this journalistic standardization.
5. Twentieth-century “good prose” inherited the newspaper model.
6. Modern software and AI extend the same homogenizing pressure.

1. `Estrangement is not new subject matter`
2. Workshop dogma says rose/grandmother/kitchen are cliche subjects.
3. Shklovsky’s move is to reject cliche as property of the object itself.
4. The object remains available.
5. What matters is treatment, placement, and perceptual freshness.
6. Therefore estrangement rescues the familiar by re-situation.

1. `Poiesis as threshold`
2. Named states such as `bud` and `rose` are nominal endpoints.
3. Real life includes the continuous transitions between them.
4. That transition zone is the site of process.
5. Heidegger’s threshold language clarifies the same point.
6. Poetry enters where definition cannot fully pin the change down.

1. `Looking -> syntax -> haunting`
2. Perception supplies the raw material.
3. Syntax is the download mechanism.
4. A shaped sentence enters the reader as pattern and cadence.
5. The result is not mere comprehension but after-effect.
6. Browning and Good Will Hunting are used to show lingering rather than hooking.

1. `Linear art -> expectation logic`
2. A sentence unfolds sequentially.
3. Sequential art creates expectations.
4. The maker can satisfy or deny those expectations.
5. Either path can produce delight and sharpen attention.
6. This is why Vuong links prose to music, film editing, DJ structure, skate videos, and AND1.

1. `Poetry/nature writing -> laboratory`
2. Plot and reportorial duty constrain sentence-level experimentation.
3. Poetry removes many of those obligations.
4. Nature writing also fails if it remains purely mimetic, since the reader has already seen the meadow.
5. Therefore both forms pressure the writer toward subjective re-seeing.
6. Baker’s mud passage is the worked example of this release.

1. `Baker mud passage -> broken mimesis`
2. Begin with plain weather and marsh description.
3. Intensify the mud through repeated, varied renaming.
4. Let the description pass from external scene into inner state.
5. The passage stops being only about mud.
6. The reader no longer sees mud as generic matter.

1. `Corral applause/moss simile`
2. Do not compare the two things by static image.
3. Compare their behavior or temporal quality.
4. “Applause” lends quickened, nebulous growth to moss.
5. The borrowed behavior changes how moss is perceived.
6. The simile works by retroactively altering the target’s motion.

1. `Synchronic vs diachronic judgment`
2. Institutions sort books within seasons, lists, and annual cycles.
3. Editing within that cycle removes idiosyncrasy for recognizability.
4. Readers do not read in that narrow frame.
5. Readers carry Shakespeare, Melville, Baldwin, Dillard, and others across time.
6. A book standardized for synchronic success may fail under diachronic reading.

1. `Use -> meaning`
2. Dictionary definition is only a snapshot.
3. Actual social use shifts what the word can do.
4. New uses produce new meanings.
5. The dictionary trails the living language.
6. Therefore writers are not merely rule-followers; they are participants in semantic change.

## Notation Choices
- Use `\text{tenor}` and `\text{vehicle}` rather than single-letter symbols; the lecture is conceptual, not algebraic.
- Use `\Longrightarrow` for explanatory or causal movement in the lecture’s argument.
- Use `\mapsto` for style-function relations such as `mimesis` or `poiesis` acting on a scene.
- Use `\leadsto` for threshold/process language where the lecture stresses becoming rather than fixed implication.
- Keep `\text{mimesis}` and `\text{poiesis}` as transliterated Greek terms, not symbolic abbreviations like `M` and `P`.
- Keep `\text{synchronic}` and `\text{diachronic}` written out in full; do not shorten to `S` and `D`.
- Treat `\text{sentence}` as the main object, not `s`, unless a later drafting pass truly needs compression.
- Use `\approx` only for evaluative slogans such as the “species never had yet” criterion; that claim is heuristic, not literal identity.
- Prefer `\text{pattern}` and `\text{expectation}` over more formal symbols like `E_n`; the lecture never formalizes them numerically.
- Do not use calculus, set-builder notation, or probability language; the lecture’s “math” is structural analogy.
- Keep percentages exactly as `80\%` and `20\%`; they are spoken proportions, not measured quantities.
- For the Lotman model, prefer ordered-pair notation:
  \[
  \text{reading} = (\text{synchronic line},\text{diachronic line})
  \]
  rather than matrix notation.
- For binary contrasts, prefer parallel displayed equations rather than tables, since no visual slide structure survives.
