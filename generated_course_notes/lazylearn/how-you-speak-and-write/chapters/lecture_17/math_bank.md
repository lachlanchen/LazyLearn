## Definitions And Objects
- `correspondence`: the rejected ideal that language can fully meet, reproduce, or carry the thing seen.
- `artifice`: the accepted constructive alternative; metaphor and formal distortion used to evoke rather than capture.
- `representation of perception`: the lecture’s preferred object of writing; not the thing itself but the felt, filtered encounter with it.
- `dash`: a fluid punctuation mark; meaning can move backward and forward through it.
- `and`: conjunctional analogue of the dash in rapid prose; used to keep momentum and suppress hierarchy among clauses.
- `prepositional ladder`: `about`, `with`, `by` as increasingly strong claims about agency, relation, and co-authorship.
- `mind’s ear`: the reader’s auditory receptivity to rhythm, sound pattern, internal rhyme, and cadence.
- `qualia`: notebook fragments of subjective perception; images, scraps of dialogue, sensory residues.
- `heart work`: later interpretive labor performed on stored images after the field encounter.
- `pattern recognition`: the stage where repeated images or motifs become structurally active across a book.
- `open hand`: principal motif in `Underland`; used as the lecture’s example of recurrence becoming form.
- `wonder`: jaw-dropped astonishment; treated as an epistemic mode rather than mere feeling.
- `unlearning`: the difficult undoing of rationalist assumptions, especially around agency and aliveness in nature.
- `portal question`: a deceptively simple question that opens into a large conceptual space.
- `sympathetic kinesthesia`: writing that works on the reader’s body first, then mind.
- `glitch`: deliberate off-balance or friction in language/form that registers strangeness rather than smoothing it away.

## Derivation Steps
`Syntax of speed`
1. Start from a sentence already in motion rather than from a neat, isolated clause.
2. Repeat `and` to avoid full stops and to suppress clause hierarchy.
3. Shorten the clauses between repeated conjunctions.
4. Increase verb pressure with more active bodily actions.
5. Drop the article in `river` to make the force feel less object-like and more immediate.
6. Result: the reader loses normal breathing rhythm and experiences a “syntax of panic.”

`Prepositional agency ladder`
1. Begin with `write about rivers`: river remains an object of description.
2. Shift to `write with rivers`: writer and river become co-thinking or co-making.
3. Shift again to `be written by rivers`: writer becomes conduit or channel.
4. Conclusion: the preposition is not decorative; it changes ontology and agency.

`Notebook-to-book pipeline`
1. Collect fast fragments during field encounters without trying to complete them.
2. Use fragments later as mnemonic ends of memory threads.
3. Pull on the thread until the surrounding scene opens back up.
4. Re-enter the scene in language.
5. Watch for cross-scene recurrences and motif formation.
6. Perform `heart work` by relating images to one another rather than merely reporting them.

`Pattern formation from repeated image`
1. A concrete image appears in one journey.
2. The same image reappears in later places and contexts.
3. The writer notices recurrence across notebooks and drafts.
4. The image is highlighted deliberately where it recurs.
5. It becomes a motif that “lights up” for writer and reader.

`Portal-question expansion`
1. Start with a question that sounds narrow and even naive.
2. Refuse the stock answer because it closes inquiry too quickly.
3. Treat the question as an entrance rather than a conclusion.
4. Pass through it into larger structure: mystery, plurality, history, metaphysics.
5. Let the question continue to organize the book without being finally exhausted.

`Wonder and scale`
1. Notice a seemingly small phenomenon.
2. Refuse to keep perception at ordinary human scale only.
3. Nest micro, human, and macro scales together.
4. Use science as enlargement of wonder, not cancellation of it.
5. Result: astonishment becomes structurally legible rather than merely emotional.

`Revision as potting`
1. Begin with a large, wet, shapeless mass of draft material.
2. Spin it into provisional form through repeated passes.
3. Shape the major vessel before ornament.
4. Add fine detail only after structural form holds.
5. Accept messiness as part of the process rather than as failure.

`Revision as mosaic`
1. Do not require linear forward writing.
2. Write set pieces wherever clarity exists.
3. Accumulate many discrete pieces.
4. Return later to arrange them into sequence and flow.
5. Use non-linearity as a constructive method, not a blockage.

`AI/Grammarly tradeoff`
1. Automated correction reduces obvious errors and lifts average prose.
2. Distinctive prose often violates default grammatical expectations on purpose.
3. Smoothing tools flag these deviations as faults.
4. Removing the deviations may improve conformity.
5. It may simultaneously damage rhythm, individuality, and formal surprise.

## Notation Choices
- Keep words primary; this lecture does not justify a heavy symbolic layer.
- Use \(\to\) for process, development, or conceptual progression.
- Use \(\neq\) or \(\not\Rightarrow\) only for strong rejections explicitly made in the lecture.
- Use \(\sim\) only for local functional equivalence, as in `and` behaving like the dash.
- Use \(\uparrow,\downarrow\) only in compact editorial schematics for intensity, frequency, or pressure; do not let them dominate the prose chapter.
- Typeset key lexical operators in roman text inside math mode: \(\text{about}, \text{with}, \text{by}, \text{and}, \text{river}\).
- Reserve \(Q\) for the one conventional quantitative item: river discharge.
- Write discharge units as \(\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}\).
- Do not introduce calculus, probability, logic symbols beyond the small editorial set above.
- Do not invent symbols for speakers, books, or motifs unless a later prompt explicitly needs a diagram.
