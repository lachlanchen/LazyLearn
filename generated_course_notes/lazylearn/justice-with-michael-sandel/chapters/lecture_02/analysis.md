# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- Sandel begins by explicitly returning from Dudley and Stephens to Bentham. The lecture first re-establishes utilitarianism in its cleanest form: maximize general welfare, understood as the balance of pleasure over pain, and then extends that principle from personal conduct to legislation and public policy.
- He immediately pivots from abstract principle to practice by translating utilitarianism into cost-benefit analysis. The movement is deliberate: once utility is defined, Sandel asks what happens when governments and firms try to operationalize it through monetary ledgers.
- The Czech smoking case is the first stress test. Sandel lets the ledger logic speak in its own terms, then exposes the disturbing implication that premature death can appear as a fiscal benefit when pensions, housing, and health costs are treated as variables in a balance sheet.
- The Ford Pinto case intensifies the same problem by introducing a more explicit monetization of life and injury. The classroom discussion then shifts from “did they calculate correctly?” to “should this kind of thing be calculated this way at all?”
- From there Sandel broadens the practical puzzle into two philosophical objections to Bentham. One concerns rights and the treatment of minorities; the other concerns commensurability, namely whether all values can be translated into one homogeneous measure.
- The Romans-and-Christians example sharpens the first objection by showing that aggregate ecstasy may still rest on a morally degrading pleasure, while the Thorndike survey sharpens the second by trying, almost absurdly, to price unlike goods on a common scale. Sandel uses both to show that Bentham’s formal clarity may conceal deeper moral losses.
- A second pivot then occurs: even if we could aggregate preferences, should we aggregate them without judging their quality? This is where Sandel turns from the arithmetic of utility to Bentham’s anti-elitist slogan that “pushpin is as good as poetry,” and the lecture moves from policy calculus to the status of higher and lower pleasures.
- Mill enters as a reforming utilitarian rather than an external opponent. Sandel first motivates Mill biographically, then presents Mill’s two repairs: the competent-judge test for higher pleasures and the claim that rights and justice can still be defended on long-run utilitarian grounds.
- The classroom experiment with Hamlet, Fear Factor, and The Simpsons is the lecture’s live test of Mill’s proposal. The lecture ends not by settling Mill’s success, but by leaving open whether Mill has really repaired utilitarianism from within or has quietly stepped beyond it, and the Bentham auto-icon anecdote serves as a coda that returns us to the strange consistency of Bentham’s outlook.

## Section Outline
1. **Returning to Bentham from the Lifeboat Case.** Open with Sandel’s recap of Dudley and Stephens and his restatement of Bentham’s highest principle: maximize utility as the balance of pleasure over pain, both for individuals and for lawmakers.
2. **Cost-Benefit Analysis as Utilitarianism in Practice.** Develop the Czech smoking example as the first worked public-policy ledger, showing how taxes, health costs, pension savings, and housing savings are all drawn into a single fiscal calculus.
3. **Ford Pinto, Cell Phones, and the Price of Life.** Use the Pinto memo and the cell-phone debate to make the lecture’s numerical spine concrete, and place a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Can human life be given a dollar value without distorting the moral problem?`
4. **Two Objections to Bentham: Rights and Commensurability.** Preserve Sandel’s own consolidation of the debate into two objections, first through Anna’s minority-rights worry and the Roman arena example, then through Thorndike’s attempt to price radically different experiences on one scale.
5. **Bentham on Quantity, Not Quality.** Introduce “pushpin is as good as poetry” as the hinge from cost-benefit arithmetic to the problem of qualitative distinctions, making clear why Bentham’s refusal to rank pleasures is initially attractive as a nonjudgmental and egalitarian stance.
6. **Mill’s Repair: Higher Pleasures and the Competent Judge.** Bring in Mill’s biography only briefly, then focus on his chapter-two test and place a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: How can a utilitarian distinguish higher from lower pleasures without leaving utilitarian premises?`
7. **Shakespeare, The Simpsons, Rights, and the Limits of the Repair.** Use the classroom experiment to test Mill’s claim, then move to Mill’s attempt to defend justice and rights as the “most sacred” part of morality before ending with the unresolved question of whether Mill has actually stepped outside utilitarianism and with Bentham’s preserved body as the lecture’s closing coda.

- [standard reconstruction] Because the transcript compresses the injury term in the Pinto calculation, present the memo as a structured table with the narrated totals rather than inventing a missing full formula from outside sources.
- [transcript-backed] Cell-phone case as a comparative balance: about 2,000 annual deaths weighed against large economic and convenience benefits; best rendered as a qualitative decision table rather than a fabricated exact equation.
- [transcript-backed] Thorndike survey ranking: Kansas at \$300,000, earthworm at \$100,000, tooth at \$4,500; include as a compact ordered table to exhibit the lecture’s challenge to commensurability.
- [standard reconstruction] Bentham’s “pushpin is as good as poetry” can be rendered schematically as: if the quantity of pleasure is equal, then the pleasures are equal in value; this should remain subordinate to the quotation and not become an overbuilt formal theory.
- [transcript-backed] Mill’s competent-judge criterion: of two pleasures, the higher is the one preferred by all or almost all who have experienced both, independent of moral pressure to prefer it.
- [standard reconstruction] A small comparison table contrasting Bentham and Mill across three axes: measure of utility, treatment of qualitative distinctions, and treatment of rights.
- [transcript-backed] The classroom experiment’s formal pattern: “most preferred” versus “highest or worthiest” should be tracked as two distinct polls, since Sandel uses the gap between them as evidence for Mill’s distinction.
- [transcript-backed] Mill’s rights claim: justice and rights stand “higher in the scale of social utility” and therefore carry more paramount obligation; this is best expressed as a conceptual ordering, not a new formula.
