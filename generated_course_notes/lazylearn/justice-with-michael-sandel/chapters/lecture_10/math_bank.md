## Definitions And Objects
- `justice`: in this lecture’s Aristotelian framing, giving people what they deserve.
- `telos`: the purpose, end, or characteristic point of a practice, institution, or good.
- `relevant excellence`: the virtue or excellence that fits the telos of the thing being distributed.
- `honorific question`: not merely who gets what, but what qualities or excellences are being recognized.
- `polis`: Aristotle’s political community; Sandel alternates between `polis`, `state`, and `political community`, but the Aristotelian term matters.
- `good life`: the end of politics for Aristotle; contrasted with mere life, exchange, or security.
- `civic virtue`: the excellence relevant to politics; includes deliberating among equals and practical wisdom.
- `happiness`: explicitly defined via Aristotle as activity of the soul in accordance with virtue.
- `practice`: the mode in which virtues are acquired; not mere rule-following but learned exercise.
- `discernment of particulars`: the practical capacity Sandel extracts from cooking, joke-telling, and music, then maps onto virtue.
- `justice as fit`: Aristotle’s later summary formula in the lecture; fitting persons and their excellences to roles.
- `Casey Martin dispute`: a rights claim that forces inquiry into the telos of golf and into what golf honors.
- `Scalia position`: rival view that games have no objective telos beyond amusement.
- `slavery conditions`: Aristotle’s own framework requires both necessity for the polis and natural fit of the slave role.

## Derivation Steps
Aristotelian distributive schema

1. Start from the weak equality claim: equal things should go to equal persons.
2. Ask the missing question: equal in what respect?
3. Answer that question by identifying the telos of the thing or practice.
4. Read off the relevant excellence from that telos.
5. Distribute the good to those who best realize that excellence.
6. Recognize that this distribution is also an act of honor.

Politics from telos to rule

1. Shift the subject matter from wealth and opportunity to offices, honors, citizenship, and rule.
2. Ask what politics is for.
3. Answer: politics aims at forming character, cultivating virtue, and realizing the good life.
4. Infer that political authority should be distributed according to contribution to that end.
5. Conclude that those who contribute most to the good life of the polis deserve greater rule and honor.
6. Add Sandel’s further point: this is not only about better outcomes, but about honoring civic excellence.

Why political life is necessary to flourishing

1. Ask why participation in politics is essential to the good life.
2. First answer: human beings are by nature political because only in a polis do we exercise language as deliberation about just and unjust.
3. Second answer: happiness is activity in accordance with virtue.
4. Virtue is not acquired from precepts alone but by practice and habituation.
5. Practical activities teach discernment of particulars; virtue belongs to that same family.
6. Therefore political deliberation is part of the practice through which human excellence is realized.

Casey Martin as a teleological rights test

1. Formulate the legal-moral question: does Casey Martin have a right to use a golf cart?
2. Notice that the answer turns on whether walking is essential to golf.
3. If walking is essential, the right claim weakens.
4. If walking is not essential, accommodation appears justified.
5. Therefore a rights dispute depends on prior argument about the telos of the game.
6. Sandel then adds the second layer: the dispute is also about what kind of excellence golf honors.

Honor in the golf case

1. Ask why elite golfers resist carts so strongly.
2. Observe that universal carts would make golf look less like an athletic competition and more like a skill game.
3. Infer that the players have a stake in the public honor attached to golf as sport.
4. Therefore the teleology debate is simultaneously an honor debate.

Slavery as internal stress test of justice as fit

1. State Aristotle’s own two conditions for just slavery: necessity and natural fit.
2. Compare those conditions to actual Athenian slavery.
3. Note that many slaves were war captives by bad luck, not “natural slaves.”
4. Conclude that Aristotle’s own application fails his criterion of fit.
5. Treat coercion as evidence that a role is not natural or fitting.
6. Use this failure to raise, but not finally settle, the larger freedom objection.

- Use `\Longrightarrow` for argumentative dependence or explanatory priority, not for strict causal law.
- Use `\Longleftrightarrow` sparingly, only where Sandel explicitly ties two sides together; in this lecture it is mainly a strong schematic compression, not a formal biconditional proof.
- Use `\approx` for lecture-level grouping or contrast classes, not for numerical approximation.
