## Definitions And Objects
- \(R_{\mathrm{nat}}\): the set of natural rights; in this lecture, life, liberty, and property.
- \(S_{\mathrm{nat}}\): shorthand for the state of nature, if a symbol is needed later.
- \(L_{\mathrm{nat}}\): shorthand for the law of nature, if a symbol is needed later.
- Unalienable right: a right that cannot be traded away, sold, or surrendered, even by its bearer.
- Property in person: Locke’s claim that each person has a property in his own person.
- Labor \(L_x\): the labor of person \(x\); used only as a compact notational aid for the labor-mixing argument.
- Unowned object \(o\in U\): an item taken from what nature has provided and left in common.
- \(P_x\): what belongs to person \(x\); use cautiously because later Locke’s text distinguishes natural property from goods defined by community law.
- Consent \(C\): the act by which people leave the state of nature and enter civil society.
- Majority consent \(C_{\mathrm{maj}}\): the collective consent of the majority, especially in the taxation discussion.
- Implied consent: tacit acceptance inferred from enjoying government services; appears in the student exchange, not as a clean Lockean axiom in the lecture.
- Arbitrary taking: a singling out at pleasure by king, parliament, or government.
- General law: a non-arbitrary rule applying by standing procedure rather than discretionary selection.
- Property as institution: Sandel’s late distinction between there being property at all and the community’s defining what counts as whose.
- “Property” in Locke’s broad sense: sometimes a global term covering life, liberty, and property together.

## Derivation Steps
**From state of nature to law of nature**
1. Begin with a pre-political condition: no government, no enacted law.
2. Call this the state of nature.
3. Say that it is a state of liberty and equality.
4. Deny that liberty here means license.
5. Introduce a law of nature that constrains action even before legislation.
6. Conclude that freedom is bounded by moral constraint, not by mere will.

**From law of nature to unalienability**
1. State the basic constraint: one cannot give up one’s own natural rights.
2. Add the paired constraint: one cannot take another’s natural rights.
3. Illustrate with life, liberty, and property.
4. Infer that even self-sale into slavery is forbidden.
5. Ground this first in God’s prior claim, then in reason.
6. Conclude that natural rights are unalienable.

**From person to labor to private property**
1. Start with property in one’s own person.
2. Move from person to labor: the labor of the body and work of the hands are properly one’s own.
3. Introduce an unowned thing left in common.
4. Say that labor mixed with that thing joins it to something already one’s own.
5. Infer a property claim over the resulting object.
6. Extend the logic from gathered goods to cultivated land.
7. Add the proviso: enough and as good must remain for others.

**From appropriation to the colonial objection**
1. Treat the labor-mixing argument as a prima facie account of original acquisition.
2. Ask whether it justifies enclosure in America.
3. Note the objection: this may rationalize appropriation from Native Americans.
4. Introduce the reply that use itself may count as labor and thus as a property claim.
5. Add the common-use / proviso reply: land held in common and land leaving too little for others cannot be taken straightforwardly.
6. Conclude that Locke’s own framework may generate pressure against easy colonial justification.

**From private enforcement to consent**
1. In the state of nature, everyone may enforce the law of nature.
2. Each person becomes judge in his or her own case.
3. Punishment tends to overshoot.
4. This produces insecurity in the enjoyment of rights.
5. People therefore consent to leave the state of nature.
6. They surrender private enforcement power to a community with legislature and majority rule.
7. Natural rights are not thereby erased.

**From consent to the taxation puzzle**
1. State the strong claim: government may not take property without consent.
2. Recall that preservation of property is the end of government.
3. Add Locke’s later claim that goods are what the law of the community says are theirs.
4. Add the tax claim: everyone should pay for protection.
5. Specify that “his own consent” becomes “consent of the majority.”
6. Infer the puzzle: individual consent seems replaced by collective consent.

**From unalienable rights to the final Lockean escape**
1. Start with the premise that unalienable rights cannot be given away.
2. Raise the problem: how can taxation or conscription be legitimate if rights remain unalienable?
3. Distinguish singling out particular persons from standing general law.
4. Treat arbitrary taking as the true violation.
5. Treat general, non-arbitrary law enacted through fair procedures as not the same kind of violation.
6. Conclude that Locke limits arbitrary power more than he limits government as such.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(R_{\mathrm{nat}}\) for natural rights and reserve it for the fixed trio life, liberty, property.
- Use words inside \(\text{...}\) for most expressions; do not introduce dense symbolic formalism for moral claims unless it clarifies a transition.
- Use \(\to\) for conceptual progression in Sandel’s exposition.
- Use \(\Longrightarrow\) for stronger normative or institutional implication.
- Use \(\neq\) for contrastive distinctions such as liberty versus license.
- Use \(x,y\) only for persons when a universal moral constraint is being stated.
- Use \(o\) only for an unowned object in the labor-mixing shorthand.
- Use \(U\) only for the common stock of unowned things if the formal shorthand is actually needed in drafting.
- Avoid heavy modal notation; the lecture’s force is better carried by plain-language operators such as \(\text{Alienate}\), \(\text{Violate}\), and \(\operatorname{Mix}\).
- Distinguish clearly between:
  - property as natural institution
  - goods or holdings defined by the law of the community
- Treat “consent” in three registers when needed:
  - \(C\): entry into civil society
  - \(C_{\mathrm{maj}}\): majority consent
  - tacit/implied consent: only in discussion, not as a primary formal term
- If “property” is used in Locke’s broad sense to include life and liberty, mark that explicitly in prose; do not assume the narrower economic sense.
