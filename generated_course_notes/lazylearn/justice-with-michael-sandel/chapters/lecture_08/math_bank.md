## Definitions And Objects
- \(P_1\): Rawls’s first principle, equal basic liberties.
- \(P_2\): Rawls’s second principle as presented in the lecture; for this lecture bank, this is the difference-principle condition on inequalities.
- Original position: the hypothetical standpoint from which principles of justice are chosen.
- Veil of ignorance: the informational restriction that removes knowledge of one’s class, talents, family background, health, and social position.
- Least well-off: the reference group used to test whether a permitted inequality is just.
- Feudal aristocracy: a regime where life prospects are fixed by birth.
- Formal equality of opportunity: jobs and careers are formally open to all.
- Fair equality of opportunity: institutions attempt to bring everyone to the same starting line.
- Meritocracy: a system that rewards performance after equalizing starting conditions as far as possible.
- Natural lottery: the unequal distribution of native talents and abilities.
- Difference principle: inequalities are acceptable only if they improve the condition of the least advantaged.
- Basic structure of society: the institutional arrangement that determines whether inequalities work to the advantage of the least well-off.
- Incentives: justified departures from equality only if needed in ways that benefit those at the bottom.
- Self-ownership objection: the libertarian claim that taxing the returns to talent violates ownership of self and one’s endowments.
- Moral desert: antecedent worthiness or deservingness.
- Entitlements to legitimate expectations: what people may rightly claim under just institutions, even without antecedent desert.
- Contribution: the meritocratic standard that Sandel says ultimately replaces pure effort.
- Supply and demand: the market mechanism that determines what talents are rewarded, without thereby conferring moral desert.

## Derivation Steps
Rawlsian choice sequence
1. Begin from the original position under the veil of ignorance.
2. Ask which principles no one would reasonably reject from that standpoint.
3. Reject utilitarianism because it permits trading off some persons for aggregate welfare.
4. Infer \(P_1\): equal basic liberties must be secured first.
5. Turn to inequalities in income and wealth only after liberties are protected.

From equality to the difference principle
1. Start with the cautious candidate: equal distribution of income and wealth.
2. Notice that even the least fortunate might do better under some inequalities.
3. Replace strict equality with a qualified principle.
4. State the qualification: an inequality is acceptable only if it improves the condition of the least well-off.
5. Apply that test to large earnings such as Jordan, Gates, Letterman, and Judge Judy.

Ladder of rival distributive views
1. Feudal aristocracy is unjust because life prospects are fixed by accident of birth.
2. Formal equality of opportunity corrects birth-fixed status by opening careers to all.
3. Formal equality remains unfair because entrants begin from unequal social starting points.
4. Fair equality of opportunity tries to equalize the starting line.
5. Meritocracy still fails because the natural lottery continues to determine winners.
6. Rawls therefore moves beyond meritocracy to the difference principle.

Incentives inside the difference principle
1. Raise the objection: high taxation may discourage productive effort.
2. Reject the crude inference that Rawls therefore requires flat equality.
3. Ask instead whether allowing incentives improves the situation of the least well-off.
4. Permit incentive-based inequalities only when that condition is met.
5. Conclude that incentives are not a decisive objection to the difference principle.

Against self-ownership as a complete answer
1. Libertarian objection: taxing gains from talent is coercive because talents belong to the individual.
2. Rawlsian reply: the natural distribution of talents is neither just nor unjust.
3. Shift the question from nature to institutions.
4. Preserve equal basic liberties while denying a privileged moral claim to all market returns from talent.
5. Conclude that institutions may regulate those returns without treating persons as mere property of the state.

Against effort as moral desert
1. Meritocratic defense says rewards track effort.
2. Rawls’s first reply: even effort and work ethic depend on contingencies such as family background and birth order.
3. Rawls’s second reply: meritocrats do not really reward effort alone.
4. The construction-worker example shows that what they actually reward is contribution.
5. Contribution itself depends on natural talent and on what society happens to prize.
6. Therefore distributive shares cannot be grounded in moral desert.

From desert to entitlement
1. Use the lottery case to show entitlement without moral desert.
2. Use the game-of-skill case to separate entitlement under rules from the further question of desert.
3. Apply that distinction to distributive justice.
4. Say that just institutions generate legitimate expectations.
5. Deny that distributive shares correspond to intrinsic worth.
6. Extend the point from earnings to educational opportunities and honors.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) for the first and second Rawlsian principles; do not introduce additional numbered principles beyond what the lecture clearly uses.
- Use words rather than abbreviations for most regimes: write “formal equality of opportunity,” “fair equality of opportunity,” “meritocracy,” and “difference principle” in full.
- Use \(\Longrightarrow\) for argumentative consequence or conceptual implication, not for strict logical proof.
- Use \(\not\Longrightarrow\) when the lecture’s point is insufficiency rather than contradiction.
- Use \(=\) only for naming or definition-level identifications, such as \(P_1=\) equal basic liberties.
- Use \(\neq\) for conceptual distinctions, especially desert versus entitlement and bottom-focused incentives versus aggregate-output reasoning.
- Use \(\Delta(\cdot)\) only in explicitly editorial reconstructions of the difference principle; mark these as reconstructions, not lecture notation.
- Keep “least well-off” as the preferred phrase rather than switching to technical alternatives like “worst-off” or “maximin” language.
- Keep “natural lottery” and “basic structure of society” as the lecture’s preferred conceptual labels.
- For salary examples, write dollar amounts in ordinary currency form rather than introducing variables unless a comparison needs compact display.
