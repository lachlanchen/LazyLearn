## Definitions And Objects
- `small geography`: the constricted social world Lee Child associates with much British crime fiction.
- `frontier-scale geography`: the larger spatial field needed for the wandering stranger / noble loner myth.
- `sense of place`: not scenic ornament, but the initial environmental condition of the story.
- `temperature`: Lee Child’s preferred controlling variable for place; includes hot/cold and hard/soft tonal contrasts.
- `key`: the musical analogue for narrative tone; used before plot is known in detail.
- `story physics`: the host’s phrase for the way setting makes some actions inevitable and others implausible.
- `pre-approval`: the reduced reader risk produced by a successful recurring character.
- `center`: habitual, skilled, literate readers.
- `outskirts`: infrequent or non-habitual readers who still need to be carried through the book.
- `propulsion`: forward movement generated by sentence rhythm, question structure, and chapter endings.
- `implied question`: a local information gap intentionally opened before the answer is given.
- `immersion`: the state of being absorbed in story, language, character, and scene.
- `monster outline`: Lee Child’s metaphor for the enormous internal structure stored through reading, despite no written outline.
- `natural dialogue`: not literal transcription of speech, but crafted dialogue that creates the illusion of speech.
- `in medias res`: beginning in the middle of things rather than with explanatory backstory.

## Derivation Steps
Place-to-plot derivation
1. The lecture rejects place as post hoc decoration.
2. A tonal condition is chosen first: hot/cold, hard/soft, one “key.”
3. A familiar location is selected that matches that condition.
4. That location makes certain actions plausible and others implausible.
5. Therefore place functions as a generative constraint on plot.

No-outline derivation
1. If the writer outlines, the writer has already told himself the story.
2. Once the story is already known, curiosity disappears.
3. Without curiosity, the writer wants the next story instead of this one.
4. Therefore Lee Child improvises as he goes.
5. This does not remove structure, because structure has been internalized through vast reading.

Audience-topology derivation
1. Readership is not monolithic.
2. There is a central band of skilled habitual readers.
3. There is a wider outer band of infrequent readers.
4. A commercially effective style must satisfy the first and assist the second.
5. Therefore style must be legible, enjoyable, and propulsive at once.

Propulsion derivation
1. A book is many thousands of sentences in sequence.
2. Each sentence therefore cannot be rhythmically inert.
3. Each sentence must tip slightly forward.
4. Cumulative forward tipping creates momentum.
5. That momentum helps readers finish the book, especially less habitual readers.

Question-hook derivation
1. Pose or imply a question.
2. Delay the answer.
3. The audience now wants resolution.
4. This desire survives even when the subject itself is minor.
5. Therefore question-making is a basic engine of plot and retention.

Remote-control derivation
1. Before remote controls, changing channels had real friction.
2. After remote controls, switching became nearly costless.
3. Low switching cost increased the need to hold attention actively.
4. Television responded with explicit question-hooks before breaks.
5. Lee Child imports that retention logic into fiction.

Dialogue derivation
1. Real speech is fragmented, repetitive, stop-start, and incoherent.
2. Written dialogue cannot simply copy that and remain readable.
3. Yet it must still sound spoken.
4. Rhythm, stress placement, and selective repetition create that illusion.
5. Therefore “natural” dialogue is carefully structured unreality.

Repetition-and-emphasis derivation
1. Repeated words persist in the ear after surrounding filler drops away.
2. In “I need my money / I lost my money,” the repeated noun anchors the rhythm.
3. The emotional state simplifies the syntax.
4. The stress naturally falls on the key words.
5. Therefore repetition can produce both emotional force and apparent naturalness.

Violence paradox derivation
1. Readers endorse law, due process, and civilized restraint.
2. Readers also experience retaliatory impulses in private frustration.
3. Fiction permits those impulses to be enacted without real-world consequences.
4. Realistic violence is brief and costly, but fiction stylizes it for release.
5. Therefore violence in narrative functions as consolation rather than documentary realism.

## Notation Choices
- Use `\Longrightarrow` for strong craft consequence or design implication, not formal proof.
- Use `\sim` for analogical mapping, especially in the music-key comparison.
- Use `\to` for ongoing local sequence, as in sentence-by-sentence propulsion.
- Use `p` only in `\Delta p_{\text{sentence}} > 0`, and label it explicitly as editorial forward pressure.
- Keep reader groups in words, `center` and `outskirts`, rather than introducing extra symbols.
- Keep `\text{book}_n` only for installment logic; do not build a larger series notation unless needed later.
- Prefer prose labels inside `\text{...}` over abstract variables, because the lecture itself is verbal and conceptual rather than symbolic.
- Do not introduce operator notation, probabilities, or optimization language not present in the lecture.
- Treat “rings of Saturn,” “story physics,” and “monster outline” as named metaphors; do not formalize them further unless the later chapter genuinely needs a schematic.
