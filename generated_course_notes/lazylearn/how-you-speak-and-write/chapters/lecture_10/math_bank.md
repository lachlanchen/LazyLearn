## Definitions And Objects
- \(\text{showing}\): prose that supplies evidence, sensory detail, action, dialogue, or thought-process so the reader infers the intended conclusion.
- \(\text{telling}\): prose that states the conclusion, summary, trait, or emotion directly.
- \(\text{evidence}\): the detail that licenses a character judgment or reader conclusion.
- \(\text{abstract label}\): a direct naming term such as “happy,” “eerie,” “beautiful,” “strange,” “angry,” “huge.”
- \(\text{concrete detail}\): an action, sensation, object, environmental trace, or verbal cue the reader can picture or otherwise experience.
- \(\text{thought verbs}\): verbs like “knew,” “thought,” “realized,” “understood,” “believed,” “wanted,” “remembered,” “imagined.”
- \(\text{camera test}\): the operational question “Can the camera see it?” used to detect abstraction.
- \(\text{cause}\): the hidden explanatory label, such as cold, hunger, lateness, blindness, anger.
- \(\text{effect}\): the outward trace, such as turning from the wind, feeling for the bench, leaves crunching, inhaling soup.
- \(\text{reader's benefit}\): exposition phrased for information delivery to the audience.
- \(\text{character's benefit}\): dialogue or description justified by what the character would naturally notice, ask, or say.
- \(\text{telegraphing}\): redundantly announcing motive or tone when the dialogue or scene already implies it.

## Derivation Steps
1. From unsupported telling to supported showing
   1. Start with a claim: a character is scared, kind-hearted, guilty, cold, tired.
   2. Ask what evidence led to that conclusion.
   3. Replace the naked claim with the evidence itself.
   4. Let the reader perform the final inference.

2. From abstract emotion to concrete action
   1. Identify the emotion word or opinion adjective.
   2. Ask whether it is directly visible or sensorily traceable.
   3. If not, replace it with behavior, environment, or bodily interaction.
   4. Keep only the details that make the same feeling legible without naming it.

3. From cause to effect
   1. Detect a causal summary such as “it was cold,” “it was late fall,” “she was blind.”
   2. Look for the local effect that such a condition would produce.
   3. Write the effect rather than the condition.
   4. Trust the reader to reconstruct the cause.

4. From generic image to immersive specificity
   1. Start with a merely visible description.
   2. Add smell, touch, sound, or texture when the scene supports it.
   3. Prefer details unique to this story over stock atmosphere markers.
   4. Use specificity to deepen rather than merely decorate the image.

5. From body-language shorthand to emotional precision
   1. Start with gesture shorthand: clenched fist, gritted teeth, crossed arms.
   2. Ask what exact thought or situation produces that gesture.
   3. Replace or supplement the gesture with thought-process, setting interaction, and verb choice.
   4. Use sequence and syntax to convey urgency or hesitation when needed.

6. From redundant dialogue labeling to shown dialogue
   1. Check whether the spoken words already imply tone or intention.
   2. Remove adverbs or explanation that merely restate that implication.
   3. Add a precise action beat or selective tag only if it sharpens the tone.
   4. Keep dialogue phrased for the character’s need, not the reader’s briefing.

7. From exposition dump to voice-filtered narration
   1. Ask whether the information is present for the reader or the character.
   2. Rephrase the material through point of view, mood, and situation.
   3. Replace dictionary-style description with in-world perception.
   4. Preserve only the amount of telling needed for pacing.
