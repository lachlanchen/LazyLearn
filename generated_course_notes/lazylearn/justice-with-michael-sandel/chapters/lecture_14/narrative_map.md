# Narrative Map
## Opening Setup

The lecture opens as a continuation: ``another doctor case.'' That matters, because Sandel is not introducing a new topic from nowhere; he is deepening an already active line of moral testing. He immediately assigns the listener a role, ``you’re a transplant surgeon,'' so the thought experiment begins not as description but as forced participation.

The opening setup is therefore pedagogically staged in second person. Before any calculation appears, we are placed inside a situation of responsibility, and only then does Sandel begin to structure the numbers that will tempt a utilitarian conclusion.

## Beat Sequence

1. **A second doctor case, now in the role of transplant surgeon.** Sandel is trying to establish continuity with an earlier medical dilemma while also making the new case more vivid by putting ``you'' inside it. It appears first because the lecture wants immediate moral involvement rather than abstract framing, and it leads into the specification of the patients whose needs define the problem.

2. **Five patients are named as urgent claimants.** He next establishes the count and the pressure: five people desperately need organ transplants to survive. This appears here because the lecture’s arithmetic has to be built from a concrete count before it can become morally tempting, and it leads into the more detailed listing of what each patient needs.  
   `Question & Answer` fit: `What is morally salient at this stage: five persons, five unmet needs, or both together?`

3. **The five are medically differentiated, then morally re-aggregated.** Sandel lists heart, lung, kidney, liver, and pancreas, which gives each patient a distinct medical need even while keeping them inside the same fatal predicament. This beat appears now because the lecture wants to preserve the concreteness of each case before the later arithmetic flattens them into a count of five, and it leads into the scarcity condition that makes the dilemma unavoidable.

4. **No donors, impending death, and therefore no easy escape.** Sandel then removes the ordinary solution: there are no organ donors, and you are about to watch the five die. This appears at exactly this moment because the lecture must foreclose the obvious non-dilemmatic alternative before introducing the morally dangerous one, and it leads directly into the healthy man in the next room.

5. **The healthy man enters and converts tragedy into temptation.** The decisive pivot is ``it occurs to you'' that a healthy man who came for a checkup is nearby. This appears here because the lecture is now ready to move from tragic scarcity to a candidate act, and it leads into the stark proposal to kill one and distribute his organs to the five.  
   `Question & Answer` fit: `Does the appearance of one healthy person make the five-to-one trade even thinkable?`

6. **The utilitarian arithmetic is made operational.** Sandel does not yet state a principle; instead he narrates an action: go in quietly, remove the five organs, one dies, five are saved. This appears here because the lecture wants the numerical advantage to arise out of a concrete act rather than a bloodless formula, and it leads into the audience test in which the calculation must confront moral resistance.  
   `Question & Answer` fit: `If one death saves five lives, what exactly is the argument for doing it?`

7. **Laughter and direct address register the shock.** The brief interruption, the joking tone, and the laughter are not accidental noise; they register the audience’s discomfort as the case crosses from medical scarcity into murder. This beat appears before the final question because the lecture wants the room to feel the repugnance that simple arithmetic cannot dissolve, and it leads into the closing demand for a show of hands.

8. **``How many would do it?'' The audience becomes part of the argument.** Sandel ends this movement by forcing a choice from the listeners rather than by summarizing the lesson himself. This appears last because the lecture has finished constructing the dilemma and now wants to expose the gap between numerical temptation and moral judgment.  
   `Question & Answer` fit: `If the count seems to favor killing one to save five, why do many people still refuse?`

## Transition Cues

Sandel’s opening pivot is continuational: ``another doctor case.'' That phrase matters because it cues us to read the scene as a further test of a line of reasoning already underway.

He then shifts quickly into second-person assignment: ``this time you’re...'' The role allocation is the first pedagogical move, and it turns the listener from spectator into agent.

The next cue is enumerative. By listing the organs one by one, he slows the scene down just enough to make the five patients concrete before letting them collapse into a number.

Scarcity is introduced with a simple elimination move: there are no donors, and you are about to see them die. This is the lecture’s way of removing escape hatches before springing the alternative.

The decisive pivot is introduced by a phrase like ``it occurs to you.'' That language marks the moral temptation as a thought arising from within the case rather than as a principle imposed from outside.

The operational move is narrated in a blunt causal sequence: take the organs, one dies, five live. Sandel thereby lets the arithmetic emerge as a possible action rather than a detached theorem.

Finally, the lecture closes this opening segment with a direct vote-inviting challenge: ``How many would do it?'' The style of the pivot is important because it turns the thought experiment into a live test rather than an already-solved example.

## Recurring Motifs

A central motif is the tension between aggregation and individuality. Sandel first gives us five patients as a count, then differentiates them medically, and then lets the count reassert itself as the lure of utilitarian reasoning.

Another recurring motif is forced role occupancy. The listener is not told about a surgeon; the listener becomes the surgeon. That device matters because it makes the arithmetic morally personal.

The lecture also works by staged removal of alternatives. First there are five needs, then no donors, then a healthy man appears. Each step narrows the situation until the numerical temptation becomes hard to ignore.

A further motif is the mismatch between countable gain and moral repugnance. The structure ``one dies, five live'' is simple enough to count, but the audience reaction suggests that counting is not the whole moral story.

The laughter is itself a motif of uneasy recognition. It signals that the room sees where the arithmetic is going before the lecturer explicitly asks them to own it.

## Pacing Risks

The biggest risk is compressing the opening into ``a surgeon can kill one to save five.'' That skips the lecture’s deliberate construction of the dilemma and destroys the pacing by which the arithmetic is made to appear persuasive.

A second risk is omitting the itemized organ list. The heart, lung, kidney, liver, and pancreas are not disposable detail; they individualize the patients before the case aggregates them.

Another common compression error is to treat ``no donors'' as background rather than as the move that removes the ordinary solution and makes the dilemma structurally necessary.

The introduction of the healthy man must not be rushed. Sandel first places him next door as an ordinary checkup patient, and only then lets the murderous possibility arise. If that sequence is flattened, the moral shock lands too weakly.

The laughter and brief interruption are easy to cut, but they matter. They register the audience’s discomfort and help explain why the final question is not merely arithmetical.

Finally, a draft writer may move too quickly from the case to a formal statement of utilitarianism. The lecture’s power lies in making the arithmetic tempting from within a narrated scene before asking whether we are willing to live by it.
