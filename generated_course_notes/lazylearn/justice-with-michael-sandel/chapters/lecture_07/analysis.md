# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- Sandel begins with a deliberate recap of the previous Kant discussion and immediately turns it into a live reconstruction: he asks the class to solve two inherited problems, how duty can be compatible with autonomy and why answering to duty is a source of dignity rather than humiliation. The first movement therefore does not merely summarize; it re-derives the core Kantian claim through Matt’s answer and then sharpens it in Sandel’s own language of self-authorship.
- He then presses the obvious objection to self-legislation: if each of us gives the moral law to ourselves, why are there not many private moral laws? Kelly’s intervention lets Sandel pivot from individual conscience to pure reason, and this pivot matters because it shifts the lecture from moral psychology to universality.
- With those preliminaries in place, Sandel names the hardest remaining question: how is a categorical imperative possible at all? This is the lecture’s first major conceptual reset, and it leads to the two-standpoint structure: sensible world versus intelligible world, necessity versus freedom.
- The two-standpoint discussion is not ornamental metaphysics; it is Sandel’s way of showing why morality cannot be empirical. He moves from the empirical-utilitarian picture of the person, to heteronomy, to the impossibility of freedom under that picture, and then to the conclusion that morality requires a standpoint from which we can regard ourselves as self-legislating.
- Once the sensible/intelligible distinction has done its work, Sandel turns to a stress test: the murderer at the door. This is a motivational transition from abstract structure to the hardest concrete case, and it lets him confront the charge that Kant’s anti-consequentialism is simply insane.
- The lecture then becomes dialectical. Sandel first rehearses Constant’s objection, then experiments with possible evasions, then isolates the crucial distinction between a lie and a misleading truth. He slows down further through the bad-tie example and the Clinton case, because these cases are meant to make us feel how strange but possibly coherent the Kantian distinction is.
- A second extended classroom exchange follows. Diana and Wesley do not merely interrupt; they force Sandel to articulate his own charitable defense of Kant: the misleading truth may still aim at deception in outcome, but it pays “homage” to duty by staying formally within the truth. This is the local conceptual obstacle that should survive in the notes as a genuine puzzle rather than being absorbed into a flat summary.
- Around 00:23:08 there is a clear lecture reset: Sandel explicitly says that last time they considered lying and now he wants to turn to Kant’s political theory. This seam should remain visible in the chapter because the lecture itself reorients from moral doctrine to political justification.
- The political-theory movement begins with Kant’s exceptional social contract, not as an actual founding event but as an “idea of reason.” Sandel then pivots to the obvious question, what moral force can such a contract have, and introduces Rawls as the modern thinker who develops this Kantian strategy in detail.
- Rawls enters through two parallels with Kant: anti-utilitarianism and the hypothetical contract. Sandel then unfolds the veil of ignorance and the original position, not yet to derive specific principles, but to explain why equality of standpoint is needed if agreement is to have justificatory force rather than merely reflect bargaining power and unequal knowledge.
- The last major movement turns from hypothetical contracts back to actual ones. Sandel asks two distinct questions—how actual contracts bind and whether they justify the terms they produce—and then uses a long series of examples to separate consent from reciprocity: lobsters, canceled contracts, marriage, baseball cards, the widow with the leaky toilet, Hume and the painter, the Indiana repairman, and marriage again.
- The lecture ends by gathering these cases into a compact moral architecture: actual contracts draw their force from autonomy and reciprocity, but real bargains are corrupted by inequalities of power and knowledge. That final consolidation then returns us to Rawls: only a hypothetical contract among equals can model justice without those distortions.

## Section Outline
1. **Duty, Autonomy, and Dignity.** Sandel opens by reconstructing Kant through student answers: duty is compatible with freedom because the law is self-imposed, and dignity lies in being the author of the law rather than merely its subject.

2. **One Moral Law and the Rule of Pure Reason.** The lecture then raises the subjectivism worry and answers it by shifting from private conscience to pure reason shared by all rational agents.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: If each of us gives the moral law to ourselves, why is there only one moral law?`

3. **Two Standpoints and the Possibility of Morality.** Sandel pivots to the hardest question—how a categorical imperative is possible—and introduces the sensible and intelligible standpoints to explain freedom, heteronomy, and the gap between is and ought.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: How can we be determined by nature and yet still be free?`

4. **The Murderer at the Door.** Constant’s objection becomes the lecture’s first great stress test of Kant: if lying is always wrong, can we still protect the innocent without abandoning the categorical imperative?

5. **Lie, Misleading Truth, and Homage to Duty.** Through the closet example, white lies, the ugly tie, and Clinton’s evasions, Sandel develops the sharp distinction between outright falsehood and truthful evasion.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: If both statements aim at deception, why is a misleading truth not morally equivalent to a lie?`

6. **Kant’s Political Theory and Rawls’s Original Position.** After an explicit reset, Sandel turns from Kant’s moral theory to his political theory, presents the social contract as an idea of reason, and then introduces Rawls’s veil of ignorance as the modern development of that strategy.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: What moral force can a hypothetical contract have if no one actually signed it?`

7. **Actual Contracts, Reciprocity, and the Limits of Consent.** The lecture then tests the moral force of real agreements through cases, distinguishing the autonomy-based force of consent from the reciprocity-based force of benefit, before returning to Rawls’s need for a hypothetical contract among equals.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Question & Answer: Is consent necessary for obligation, or can reciprocal benefit bind us even without agreement?`

- The lecture contains a clear internal splice around 00:23:08: the first movement completes the Kant/lying discussion, and the second begins with “Last time, we talked...” before turning to political theory. Preserve this reset rather than smoothing it away.
- The transcript around 00:24:27–00:25:35 contains obvious duplication and garbling (“would have different interests” and repeated fragments about laws being created). Reconstruct only the stable argumentative point: actual conventions reflect unequal interests, bargaining power, and knowledge, and therefore do not automatically yield just laws.
- The phrase “a misleading truth pays a certain homage to duty” is Sandel’s interpretive defense of Kant, not a canonical formula from Kant himself; the notes should preserve the thought but keep the attribution clear.
- There are no validated board equations or slide diagrams for this lecture, so every displayed equation will be a transcript-based or cautiously reconstructed schematic rather than a recovered visual transcription.
- The moral distinction between lie and misleading truth should not be collapsed into consequences alone; Sandel’s whole point is that the dispute turns on motive, formal adherence to the moral law, and what one can control.
- In the contract half of the lecture, keep separate the two questions Sandel distinguishes: how contracts bind and whether they justify the terms they produce. Flattening those into one “social contract theory” paragraph would lose the lecture’s real argumentative skeleton.
- Several later examples are pedagogical rather than doctrinally neat: the marriage example is partly comic, the repair-van case is autobiographical, and the Hume case is historical. Normalize noisy phrasing, but keep the function of each case in the argument.
- The chapter should end openly, as the lecture does, by using Rawls’s hypothetical contract to set up the next question—what principles of justice would actually be chosen—rather than pretending that this lecture already answers it.
