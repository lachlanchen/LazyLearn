# Narrative Map
## Opening Setup
Sandel begins by returning us to the unresolved problem left by Locke in the previous lecture: if political authority is grounded in consent, what can even a majority not rightly do? He does not restart from first principles; instead, he narrows the lens from taxation, already discussed, to conscription, because the move from property to life makes the issue of self-ownership morally sharper.

The opening therefore has a clear pedagogical function. We are first reminded that Locke already allowed taxation without fresh individual consent, and then we are pushed to ask whether the same logic can really reach as far as compulsory military service. That setup gives the lecture its initial tension and makes the rest of the hour read as an investigation of consent under pressure.

## Beat Sequence
1. **Recap Locke, then intensify the case from property to life.** Sandel is trying to establish that the real problem is not taxation but whether consent can authorize commands over life and death; this appears first because it inherits the exact unresolved question from the previous lecture. It leads naturally into Locke’s answer, since the audience now needs to see whether self-ownership blocks conscription or not. `[Q&A]`

2. **State Locke’s answer through the contrast between legitimate command and arbitrary power.** Sandel uses the sergeant example to show that Locke allows even drastic military command, provided authority is not arbitrary; this appears here because the lecture needs a definite answer before moving into present-day cases. The contrast between commanding danger and taking a penny corruptly then opens the broader question of why consent has such force in the first place.

3. **Shift from theory to a concrete recruitment problem.** Sandel introduces Iraq and the failure to meet recruitment targets in order to turn an abstract Lockean issue into an immediate policy choice; this appears at exactly the moment when the lecture needs something concrete enough for polling and argument. The three options set up the decision structure that the classroom debate will test.

4. **Use the Civil War hybrid system to surface the coercion objection.** By moving from a simple poll to the draft-plus-buyout system, Sandel is trying to show that market freedom in military service may hide deep unfairness; this appears now because the initial menu of options needs a morally revealing case, not just preferences. It leads to the first major conceptual move: voluntary exchange may not be fully free when background inequality is doing the work. `[Q&A]`

5. **Let Emily generalize the objection from the Civil War case to the all-volunteer army.** Sandel is trying to prevent the class from treating the Civil War system as a historical oddity by asking whether the same criticism reaches the modern paid army; this appears here because the first objection has to be tested for scope before the lecture can move on. It leads into the deeper comparison between draft, paid service, and market allocation more generally. `[Q&A]`

6. **Pivot from coercion to patriotism and civic obligation.** Sandel now tries to establish that there is a second objection distinct from unfairness: perhaps military service is the wrong sort of thing to value mainly through wages and choice; this appears here because the class has reached the limit of the inequality argument and needs another axis of evaluation. It leads into the first explicit synthesis, where he steps back and names the two objections side by side. `[Q&A]`

7. **Step back and formulate the two unanswered questions.** Sandel is trying to show that the discussion has generated two live philosophical problems, not just a pile of opinions: what inequalities undermine freedom, and what obligations citizenship imposes; this appears here because the first half of the lecture has to be harvested before the topic shifts. It leads into the bridge to reproduction by carrying the same consent-and-market framework into a new domain.

8. **Bridge from military service to reproductive markets, then narrow to Baby M.** Sandel is trying to show that the question about what money should buy is not confined to war but reaches into procreation, egg markets, sperm banks, and finally surrogacy; this appears now because the lecture wants a second arena where consent, price, and moral meaning collide. It leads into a new classroom dispute structured like the first half: contract defense first, objections second.

9. **Replay the pattern through surrogacy: contract, flawed consent, then commodification.** Sandel first establishes the contractarian defense that a deal is a deal, then lets objections emerge from lack of information and from the thought that baby-selling or selling maternal rights is dehumanizing; this appears here because the second half mirrors and deepens the first. It leads to the court decision and to the more abstract philosophical vocabulary needed to describe what is wrong. `[Q&A]`

10. **Move from courtroom reasoning to philosophical generalization.** Sandel is trying to show that the court’s two reasons, tainted consent and commodification, open onto a broader claim associated with Elizabeth Anderson: some goods are misvalued when treated as objects of use and profit; this appears last because only after the concrete case has been fully aired can the lecture widen into theory again. It leads not to closure but to an open ending: what goods, if any, should money not buy?

## Transition Cues
- Sandel often closes one phase with a crisp marker before opening the next: `So much for taxation` is the model case.
- He likes to sharpen an abstract point by asking, in effect, `But what about the harder case?`
- He pivots into examples with phrases like `Let’s take the case...` or `Consider three policies...`, which signal that theory is about to be stress-tested.
- He uses the poll not just to gather opinion but to create argumentative pressure: `How many favor... ?` is a transition into dialectic, not mere classroom management.
- He frequently turns a student claim into a broader challenge: `What do you say to that?`, `Who has a reply?`, `Doesn’t that also trouble you...?`
- When he wants to synthesize, he steps back explicitly: `Let’s see what we’ve learned` or `We’ve really heard two arguments`.
- The bridge into the second half is characteristic: a provocative small question, `Should you be able to bid for a baby?`, widens into a new field of cases.
- Near the end he shifts from case analysis to philosophical abstraction with cues like `One of the philosophers we read...` and `What does it mean to say... ?`

## Recurring Motifs
- **Consent under strain.** Sandel keeps asking not whether consent matters, but when it is morally sufficient and when it is compromised.
- **Concrete case before abstraction.** We move from Locke to Iraq, from policy options to the Civil War system, from egg and sperm markets to Baby M, and only then to Anderson.
- **Two-objection structure.** In both halves of the lecture, the first objection concerns freedom or tainted consent, while the second concerns moral meaning or commodification.
- **Classroom dialectic as method.** Student answers are not side material; Sandel uses them to generate the next distinction.
- **Step back and restate.** After a stretch of exchange, he periodically names the larger issue we have actually uncovered.
- **Open endings.** He does not resolve the deepest questions on the spot; he sharpens them and carries them forward.

## Pacing Risks
- A draft writer may compress the Locke recap too quickly and lose why conscription is the decisive harder case after taxation.
- The recruitment slide and poll can easily be treated as scene-setting only, but they are structurally important because they generate the Civil War hybrid case.
- The Civil War discussion should not be reduced to `market bad because unequal`; the lecture slowly builds that objection through Liz, Sam, Raul, and Emily.
- Emily’s challenge is easy to flatten into the earlier coercion objection, but it matters because it extends the criticism to the all-volunteer army and keeps the first half moving.
- The patriotism thread should not be merged into the coercion thread; Sandel is careful to separate unfairness from the claim that military service may be the wrong kind of good for market allocation.
- The bridge from military service to reproduction can feel abrupt if the writer omits the `bid for a baby` pivot and the egg/sperm market setup.
- In the surrogacy half, the temptation is to jump straight to the court ruling; doing so loses the classroom rhythm in which contractarian confidence is first granted and then destabilized.
- The final move to Anderson should not come too early. If abstracted prematurely, the lecture loses the way the courtroom and classroom arguments prepare the need for a theory of commodification.
- The garbled stretch around the court’s second objection makes it tempting to over-smooth the passage; the safer approach is to preserve the double movement from `imperfect information` to `dehumanization` without pretending the transcript is cleaner than it is.
