# Narrative Map
## Opening Setup
Sandel opens by making the confrontation explicit: we are leaving behind Kantian and Rawlsian attempts to detach justice from virtue and desert, and turning to Aristotle’s contrary claim that justice gives people what they deserve. He immediately frames the lecture around a method rather than a doctrine alone: to reason about justice, we must reason about purpose, end, or telos.

The opening is compact but loaded. He gives us one clean formula, one immediate difficulty, and one miniature example, so that the rest of the lecture can unfold as a series of increasingly demanding applications rather than as a static summary of Aristotle.

## Beat Sequence
1. **Turn to Aristotle as a challenge to modern justice.** Sandel first establishes the target: modern theories try to separate justice and rights from moral desert, while Aristotle refuses that separation. This appears at the start because the lecture needs a clear adversarial frame, and it leads directly to the first pressure point: if justice means giving equals equal things, we must still ask equal in what respect. `[Q&A: Equal things to equal persons, but equal in what respect?]`

2. **Flutes as the lecture’s compact model of teleological justice.** He introduces the flute case to show, in the simplest possible setting, that the relevant standard of distribution comes from the purpose of the good being distributed. This appears here because Sandel wants a low-stakes illustration before moving into politics, and it leads to the deeper claim that debates about purpose are also debates about what excellences deserve honor. `[Q&A: Why should the best flutes go to the best flute players?]`

3. **From teleology in general to two proving grounds: politics and golf.** Once the flute case has fixed the method, Sandel widens the scope and says teleology is hard to avoid in ethics and politics, then announces two applications: Aristotle’s politics and the Casey Martin golf-cart case. This appears at exactly the right moment because the lecture now has to test whether the elegant flute example survives contact with social institutions, and it leads first into politics as the main constructive case.

4. **Politics reframed as offices, honors, and rule rather than wealth.** Sandel contrasts modern distributive justice, usually about income, wealth, and opportunity, with Aristotle’s focus on who should rule, who should be a citizen, and how authority should be distributed. This appears here to relocate the subject matter before the theory is applied, and it leads into the central question: what is politics for, if its distributive principle is to be derived teleologically? `[Q&A: If politics is for the good life, who should rule?]`

5. **The telos of politics and the Rawlsian worry.** Sandel answers that politics is for forming character, cultivating virtue, and realizing the good life, then immediately stages the modern objection that politics should not make us good but should protect our freedom to choose our own ends. This beat appears here so that Aristotle’s thesis is not merely asserted but pressured at once, and it leads into the motivational question that becomes the core of the middle of the lecture: why is participation in politics essential to human flourishing at all?

6. **Two answers to why political participation matters.** Sandel first argues from the Politics that human beings realize their nature only in a polis through language and deliberation about justice; then he deepens the answer from the Nicomachean Ethics by insisting that virtue and happiness require practice, habituation, and discerning particulars, not just memorizing rules. This appears here because the lecture must connect the telos of politics to the structure of human life, and it leads back from anthropology and ethics to institutional distribution: if civic virtue is acquired this way, those best at it seem to deserve political honor and authority. `[Q&A: Why can’t a person live a fully good life outside politics?]`

7. **Politics culminates in the joint teleological and honorific claim.** Sandel returns from the long motivational detour to Pericles and civic excellence: rule should go not only to those whose judgment produces better outcomes, but also to those whom politics is properly meant to honor. This appears here because the politics example now has to be closed as a full application of Aristotle’s method, and it leads into the contemporary golf case by showing that teleology and honor travel together.

8. **Casey Martin as a rights dispute that turns into a telos dispute.** Sandel introduces the lawsuit, polls the room, and lets students argue both sides, so that the class itself feels the transition from “does he have a right?” to “what is golf for?” This appears here because he wants a modern controversy to show Aristotle still has bite, and it leads to Scalia’s dissent, which gives Sandel a fully explicit anti-Aristotelian rival view. `[Q&A: Does Casey Martin have a right to use a golf cart, or does that depend on the purpose of golf?]`

9. **Scalia, the recap seam, and the second pass through golf.** Sandel first presents Scalia’s view that games are for amusement and have no discoverable essence, then later resumes with “When we ended last time,” recaps the cart issue, and uses Jenny’s proposal to force the class to see that fairness alone does not settle the matter. This appears when it does because the lecture wants both the judicial argument and the classroom objection cycle, and it leads to Sandel’s two explicit morals: the Casey Martin right depends on whether walking is essential to golf, and that dispute is also about whether golf is honored as a genuine athletic competition. `[Q&A: Would allowing everyone to use a cart solve the fairness problem, or would it change the game?]`

10. **Final evaluative turn: freedom, slavery, and the unresolved clash with Kant and Rawls.** Only after politics and golf are in place does Sandel raise the major objection: if justice is a matter of fit between persons and roles, does that leave room for freedom? This appears late because he wants the objection to answer a developed Aristotelian view rather than a caricature, and it leads through the hard case of slavery and the student objections into the lecture’s real endpoint: the unresolved question whether the right is prior to the good, and whether freedom means choosing one’s ends or discovering one’s nature. `[Q&A: If justice depends on telos and fit, what room is left for freedom and individual choice?]`

## Transition Cues
- Sandel likes to pivot by saying, in effect, “yes, but the real question immediately arises,” so the lecture repeatedly moves from a formula to the missing discriminating criterion.
- He uses “now let’s see how that works” to shift from abstract claim to worked example; this is the engine of the move from flutes to politics and later from politics to golf.
- He often plants an objection with “you might worry” or “you might say,” which means the text should preserve the objection as part of the forward motion rather than folding it into later summary.
- He marks deepening rather than replacement with phrases like “he gives two answers” or “there’s a further feature,” so the draft should show cumulative structure instead of treating each new point as a fresh start.
- The golf case is introduced with a demonstrative cue, essentially “here’s an example,” signaling that the modern controversy is meant to illuminate the Aristotelian framework, not distract from it.
- The mid-lecture seam matters: “when we ended last time” is not filler but a real recap pivot, and it should survive as a visible reset in the chapter.
- Near the end he turns reflective with “now I want to consider whether Aristotle is right,” which signals a change from exposition of Aristotle to evaluation of Aristotle.
- He closes by converting conclusions into next questions, effectively saying: we have reached the place where the real dispute with Kant and Rawls can now begin.

## Recurring Motifs
- Justice requires a relevant criterion, not bare equality.
- Telos is the missing middle term between a good and its just distribution.
- Distribution and honor are intertwined; who gets what and what excellence is being recognized are asked together.
- Politics is treated not as neutral rule-framing but as character-forming association ordered to the good life.
- Human capacities are realized in practice and shared deliberation, not merely in abstract rule-knowledge.
- Modern rights talk repeatedly reappears, not as a straw man but as the standing alternative Aristotle must answer.
- The lecture keeps turning apparently practical disputes into questions about the nature of the practice itself.
- The final axis of conflict is stable throughout: fit and teleology versus freedom and choice.

## Pacing Risks
- A draft is very likely to compress the flute example into a slogan and miss its real job, which is to install the lecture’s method before the harder cases begin.
- It would be easy to flatten the politics section into “Aristotle says politics aims at the good life,” but Sandel spends real time motivating why that claim might seem worrying from a Kantian or Rawlsian perspective.
- The two answers to why political participation matters should not be merged too quickly; Sandel first argues from human nature and language, then from virtue as practice and habituation.
- The cooking, flute-playing, and joke-telling analogies may look expendable, but they carry the lecture’s account of how virtue is learned and why book learning is insufficient.
- The Pericles passage should not be reduced to a simple consequentialist claim about better judgment; Sandel insists that the honorific reason is a distinct further reason.
- The first pass through the golf case and the second pass after the recap seam should not be collapsed into one block. The lecture deliberately lets the issue rest, resumes it, and sharpens it.
- Jenny’s universal-cart proposal is easy to summarize away, but it is one of the clearest places where the lecture stages a live tension and should therefore survive as a standalone `Question & Answer`.
- The slavery discussion should not be treated as a detachable historical aside; in Sandel’s sequencing it is the sharpest route into the freedom objection.
- The ending is not a conclusion in the usual sense. If the draft sounds settled at the end, it will have lost the lecture’s actual rhythm, which is to end by opening the larger confrontation with Kant and Rawls.
