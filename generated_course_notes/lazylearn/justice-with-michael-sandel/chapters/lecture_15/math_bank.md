## Definitions And Objects

- `P(\text{fair})`, `P(\text{unfair})`
  Poll shares in the room for the opening question.

- `Rooney's service`
  Entertainment football performed at a level few others can match; linked in the transcript to pleasure and willingness to pay.

- `care worker service`
  Care labor described as socially valuable and, in Lois's argument, often life-sustaining.

- `scarce talent`
  The economist's supply-side descriptor for Rooney's abilities.

- `willingness to pay`
  The pro-market indicator used to connect pleasure, ticket prices, and high earnings.

- `equal chance in life`
  Lois's background-condition test for whether pay inequalities could count as fair.

- `social contribution`
  The alternative evaluative standard invoked against pure market valuation.

- `law of supply and demand`
  The economist's explanatory model for why Rooney earns more and care workers earn less.

- `fairness`
  The normative target of the lecture; explicitly challenged as distinct from market outcome.

## Derivation Steps

1. Poll to numerical split
   1. The lecture opens with a binary question: fair or unfair.
   2. The audience votes.
   3. Sandel reports `46\%` fair and `54\%` unfair.
   4. Therefore the room is divided and discussion is warranted.

2. Pleasure argument to high pay
   1. Rooney provides a service many people greatly enjoy.
   2. People are willing to pay a great deal for that service.
   3. Very few people can provide the same service at Rooney's level.
   4. Therefore Rooney earns a lot.
   5. Candidate compact form:
      \[
      \text{pleasure} + \text{willingness to pay} + \text{scarcity}
      \Longrightarrow
      \text{high earnings}.
      \]

3. Equality objection to fairness objection
   1. If everyone had an equal chance in life, pay differences might be fair.
   2. Lois denies that current differences are fair on that scale.
   3. She adds that care worker services are indispensable in a way football entertainment is not.
   4. Therefore market reward may diverge from what matters most socially.

4. Social value argument
   1. Many people would be worse off, even dead, without care work.
   2. Football gives pleasure; care gives care.
   3. Therefore social importance may rank care above entertainment.
   4. This supports:
      \[
      \text{social contribution}
      \stackrel{?}{>}
      \text{market reward}.
      \]

5. Economist reply
   1. Care work is valuable.
   2. But many people are willing and able to do it.
   3. Therefore a lower wage is sufficient to elicit supply.
   4. Rooney's skill is rarer.
   5. Therefore Rooney's wage is higher.
   6. Candidate compact form:
      \[
      \text{scarcity} \Longrightarrow \text{higher wage}, \qquad
      \text{abundant supply} \Longrightarrow \text{lower wage}.
      \]

6. Susan's distinction
   1. Supply and demand may explain what wages are.
   2. Explanation of wage formation does not yet answer whether the outcome is fair.
   3. Therefore:
      \[
      \text{supply and demand}
      \neq
      \text{fairness}.
      \]

## Notation Choices

- Use `\text{...}` for all displayed expressions; the lecture's formal content is conceptual and quantitative, not algebraic in a technical sense.

- Use `P(\text{fair})` and `P(\text{unfair})` for the poll shares rather than raw labels like `A` and `B` once the chapter begins discussing results.

- Use `\Longrightarrow` for argumentative consequence, not causal law.

- Use `\stackrel{?}{=}` for contested equivalence claims such as
  \[
  \text{market wage} \stackrel{?}{=} \text{moral desert}.
  \]

- Use ordinary `\neq` for the stronger Susan-style distinction:
  \[
  \text{supply and demand} \neq \text{fairness}.
  \]

- Keep the slide prompt itself in full text form when first introduced, since it is frame-visible and structurally important.

- Do not introduce formal demand curves, supply curves, or variables like `w`, `D`, `S` unless later transcript evidence supports them.
