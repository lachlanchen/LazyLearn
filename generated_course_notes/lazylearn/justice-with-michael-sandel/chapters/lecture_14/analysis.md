# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc

The lecture opens in medias res with a second ``doctor case,'' so the chapter should begin by preserving that sense of continuation rather than pretending this is a fresh abstract problem. Sandel immediately places us in a forced-choice scenario: we are transplant surgeons, five patients each need a different organ, and without intervention they will die.

He then sharpens the scenario in two steps. First, the five are medically distinct but morally aggregated: heart, lung, kidney, liver, pancreas. Second, there are no donors, which removes the easy solution and makes the arithmetic of loss unavoidable. This is the first pivot from clinical setup to moral pressure.

The next transition is the key one. Sandel introduces a healthy man in the next room, initially as an ordinary checkup patient, then as the possible source of the five needed organs. The lecture thereby moves from tragic scarcity to an active proposal: sacrifice one to save five.

Only after that proposal is fully in place does Sandel force the utilitarian structure into the open. The scenario now has a simple countable form, and the audience is pressed with the live question of whether the surgeon should do it. The lecture’s real work is to make the numerical attraction of the choice vivid before testing whether that attraction is morally decisive.

The final beat is pedagogical rather than expository: ``How many would do it?'' The lecture ends this opening movement by turning the thought experiment back onto the audience. That question should survive in the chapter as a structural hinge, because it marks the passage from setup to evaluative argument.

## Section Outline

1. **Another Doctor Case: The Transplant Surgeon’s Situation.** Open with the sense that Sandel is continuing an earlier sequence of medical thought experiments, then set out the case of the transplant surgeon and the five patients in need of organs.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `What exactly is the morally relevant structure of the case: five urgent needs, or five separate persons?`

2. **Five Different Organs, No Donors.** Keep the lecture’s move from general urgency to concrete medical specification: heart, lung, kidney, liver, pancreas, and no donors available. This section should make clear why the problem is being staged so that mere waiting is morally equivalent to letting the five die.

3. **The Healthy Man in the Next Room.** Introduce the ordinary checkup patient as the decisive pivot that turns tragedy into temptation.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `Does the appearance of one healthy person convert the problem into a permissible trade of one for five?`

4. **The Utilitarian Arithmetic of Sacrifice.** This is where the chapter should isolate the lecture’s mathematical spine: one death, five lives saved, apparent net gain. The notes should not endorse the arithmetic, but they should present it clearly enough that the later moral resistance has something definite to answer.

5. **Would You Do It? Audience Confrontation as Argument.** End this opening chapter movement with Sandel’s direct question to the audience, since that is how he converts an abstract calculation into a personal moral test.  
   `Question & Answer` subsection: `If the arithmetic favors killing one to save five, why do many people still resist it?`
