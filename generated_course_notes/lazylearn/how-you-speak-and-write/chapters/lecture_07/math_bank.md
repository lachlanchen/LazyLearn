## Definitions And Objects
- `first draft`: the initial full manuscript shown on one screen.
- `reader comments`: external notes from trusted readers or collaborators who have seen the first draft.
- `second draft`: the rewritten book in progress, composed anew rather than line-edited from the first draft.
- `revision priority`: the ordering principle by which some comments rise to the top because they diagnose structural weaknesses.
- `plot twist interval`: Follett's working pacing heuristic that important surprises should recur roughly every three to four pages.
- `ponderous opening`: a first chapter whose pace is too slow to draw the reader in quickly.
- `rewrite from scratch`: Follett's method of retyping the book anew instead of marking changes onto the previous version.
- `time budget`: the explicit decomposition of the book into planning, first-draft, and second-draft years.
- `severe doubt`: the recurring mid-project state in which the author questions whether anyone would want to read the book.
- `press on`: the operational response to doubt; continue writing rather than stopping for existential judgment.
- `standards`: Follett's quality threshold, explicitly distinguished from a publisher's desire to publish earlier.
- `absorption in work`: the closing emotional state that explains why the long process is sustainable.

## Derivation Steps
Feedback to diagnosis:
1. Start from a reader comment: `I was not drawn in as quickly`.
2. Treat the comment as evidence about reader experience, not as a personal slight.
3. Infer that the early chapters may be too slow.
4. Translate slowness into a structural claim: the opening may be ponderous.
5. Compare that diagnosis with the pacing heuristic.
6. Promote the comment to high revision priority.

Pacing heuristic:
1. Ask what a comment about slow reading speed really signals.
2. Recall the preferred rhythm of surprises in the manuscript.
3. State the local rule: a plot twist every three to four pages.
4. Infer that missing this rhythm can weaken reader pull.
5. Use the rule diagnostically, not dogmatically.

Second-draft method:
1. Begin with the first draft and a list of comments.
2. Refuse the surface-edit strategy of simply marking corrections.
3. Place the prior material behind or beside the new writing process.
4. Recompose the book by typing the whole draft again.
5. Use old material and comments as guides rather than as the editable object.
6. Conclude that the second draft is a new construction.

Time-budget decomposition:
1. Assign one year to planning.
2. Assign one year to the first draft.
3. Assign one year to the second draft.
4. Sum the stages into the total horizon of the book.
5. Use the decomposition to justify the scale of the process.

Necessity of the second draft:
1. Raise the challenge that the extra draft may be unnecessary.
2. Note that some publishers would accept the first draft.
3. Reinterpret that willingness as lower standards, not sufficient quality.
4. Compare first and second draft outcomes.
5. Conclude that rewriting from scratch yields a better product.

Crisis-response rule:
1. Recognize the recurring moment deep into the book.
2. Notice that several hundred pages have already been written.
3. Name the resulting reaction as severe doubt, not full collapse.
4. Refuse to stop the process for that feeling alone.
5. Press on.
6. Reserve judgment for later revision: if it is not good enough, rewrite it.

## Notation Choices
- Use `\text{...}` throughout; this lecture's mathematics is procedural and conceptual, not symbolic in the usual physical sense.
- Use `\to` for workflow progression or movement from one stage to another.
- Use `\Rightarrow` for diagnostic implication, practical consequence, or operational rule.
- Use `\approx` only for the pacing heuristic `3\text{--}4` pages, since the rule is approximate and method-specific.
- Use `=` only for the explicit year allocations and for Follett's strong formulation that the second draft is a rewritten book.

- Use subscripts only where they clarify process time, e.g. `T_{\text{plan}}`, `T_{\text{draft1}}`, `T_{\text{draft2}}`.
- Keep process nouns concrete and stable: `first draft`, `reader comments`, `second draft`, `rewrite from scratch`, `severe doubt`.
