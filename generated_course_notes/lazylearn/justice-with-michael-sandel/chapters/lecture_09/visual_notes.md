# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- After the sponsor material and a brief carryover from the previous discussion, Sandel reopens with Rawls’s distinction between moral desert and entitlements to legitimate expectations, then pivots from income and wealth to opportunities, hiring, and admissions. The move is deliberate: affirmative action is introduced as a new arena in which the same deeper question about desert will be tested.
- He first builds the Cheryl Hopwood case concretely: her educational path, her grades, her test performance, the University of Texas policy, and the law school’s explicit appeal to demographic diversity. This grounds the lecture in a specific grievance before any abstract principle is named.
- Sandel then suspends the legal question and turns it into a moral one: is the policy fair, and does Hopwood have a legitimate complaint? The room becomes a split jury, which lets him draw out objections and defenses in a genuinely dialogical order rather than presenting a ready-made taxonomy.
- The first pivot comes from Bree’s arbitrariness objection to Anisha’s reply about unequal educational opportunity. Sandel sharpens Anisha’s point by reframing it: perhaps test scores and grades do not transparently measure academic promise when background inequalities distort what those numbers mean.
- He next broadens the debate beyond educational disadvantage. David introduces a reparative or compensatory defense; Kate resists by saying we should fix causes, not outcomes; Hannah brings in nepotism and legacy admissions; Ted returns to the claim that race-based decisions are inherently unfair. The lecture’s rhythm here is cumulative: each intervention adds a new principle, not just another opinion.
- Only after this accumulation does Sandel stop and classify what has emerged. He names three arguments for affirmative action: corrective, compensatory, and diversity. This summary beat is one of the lecture’s main structural hinges and should remain visible in the final notes.
- He then treats the compensatory argument first and immediately exposes its hardest objection: is it fair to ask Hopwood to bear costs for wrongs in which she was not personally implicated? That question raises collective responsibility across time, and Sandel explicitly sets it aside in order to focus on the diversity argument.
- The diversity argument is then split in two: diversity for the sake of the educational experience of all students, and diversity for the sake of the wider civic mission of institutions that train leaders. This leads naturally to the Bakke case and Harvard’s brief, where race is defended as one “plus” factor among many sources of diversity.
- Once the Harvard rationale is on the table, Sandel pivots back to Rawls. The key question becomes whether common-good reasoning may deny admission to an individual for the sake of a social mission, or whether an applicant has a right to be judged only by achievement and effort.
- The lecture then makes a deeper Rawlsian move: perhaps no one morally deserves admission under a supposedly neutral standard, because standards themselves depend on the mission an institution defines for itself. This carries the discussion from a complaint about unfair treatment back to the earlier question of desert versus entitlement.
- Around 00:25:52 there is a visible seam: the flute example appears briefly, then the lecture resets at 00:26:20 with “When we ended last time.” That reset should be preserved rather than edited away, because it functions as a recap before the next philosophical escalation.
- The next major transition comes from Da’s objection to Hannah’s claim that a private college may define its mission however it wants. Sandel tests that view by comparing present-day diversity rationales with the University of Texas Law School’s segregationist mission in the 1950s and Harvard’s anti-Jewish quotas in the 1930s.
- Hannah’s reply in terms of inclusion rather than exclusion, and the subsequent discussion of malice, do not settle the issue. Sandel pushes further: even without malicious judgment, are applicants still being used instrumentally for a wider purpose, and what does that imply about whether admissions can be a matter of moral desert?
- From this pressure point he turns explicitly to Aristotle, the thinker who refuses the Rawlsian detachment of justice from desert. The flute example, then the tennis-court example, lets him unfold Aristotle’s idea that justice depends on the relevant excellence and the telos of the thing being distributed.
- The lecture ends by recasting the earlier affirmative-action dispute as a disagreement about the purpose of a university education. Aristotle is not appended as a new topic; he is brought in as the philosopher whose teleological view makes sense of why the admissions debate is really a debate about the end of the practice itself.

## Section Outline
1. **From Rawls to Hopwood.** Open by recalling Rawls’s distinction between moral desert and legitimate expectations, then move directly into the Cheryl Hopwood case so that the chapter begins where the lecture begins: a concrete dispute framed by a prior philosophical distinction.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Does Cheryl Hopwood have a legitimate complaint if applicants with lower academic indices were admitted under a race-conscious policy?`

2. **Arbitrariness, Unequal Preparation, and Measured Potential.** Follow Bree’s claim that race is an arbitrary factor with Anisha’s reply that scores and grades may mismeasure real academic promise under unequal schooling. Keep the lecture’s rhythm of objection, reply, and Sandel’s clarifying restatement.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Can affirmative action be justified as a correction for the distorted meaning of grades and test scores?`

3. **Compensation, Legacy, and the Diversity Turn.** Let the classroom debate widen from reparations and past injustice to nepotism, legacy admissions, and the educational value of diversity, without collapsing these into a single defense. The point of the section is to show how several rival justifications are generated before Sandel names them.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Is affirmative action compensation for past injustice, and if so is that fair to present applicants?`

4. **Three Arguments and the Harvard/Bakke Rationale.** Preserve Sandel’s explicit mid-lecture classification into corrective, compensatory, and diversity arguments, then develop the two forms of the diversity argument through the University of Texas and Harvard/Bakke materials. This section should feel like the lecture’s conceptual map coming into focus.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `What exactly are the three distinct arguments for affirmative action, and how does the diversity argument differ from the other two?`

5. **Rights, Mission, and the Historical Challenge.** Begin from the Rawlsian objection that common-good arguments do not automatically override rights, then preserve the reset seam and Da’s challenge to mission-based admissions criteria. Sandel’s comparison with segregated Texas and anti-Jewish Harvard should be treated as the lecture’s sharpest test of whether institutional purpose can justify admissions rules.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Is there a principled difference between today’s diversity rationale and earlier exclusionary appeals to institutional mission?`

6. **Aristotle, Flutes, and Teleological Justice.** The final section should turn fully to Aristotle: justice as giving people their due, the relevance of the thing distributed, the flute example, the rejection of irrelevant criteria, and the role of telos. End by showing that the affirmative-action debate can be recast as a disagreement about the purpose of university education.  
   Standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: `Why should the best flutes go to the best flute players, and why is that answer not utilitarian?`
