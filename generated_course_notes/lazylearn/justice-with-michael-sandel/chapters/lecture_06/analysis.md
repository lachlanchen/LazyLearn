# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- Sandel opens by announcing a difficult turn: Kant is the hardest philosopher in the course, but worth the effort because his moral philosophy addresses two large questions at once, the supreme principle of morality and the meaning of freedom. He briefly humanizes Kant with biographical detail, then pivots immediately to the philosophical stake.
- The first sustained movement opposes Kant to utilitarianism. Sandel argues that dignity does not rest on self-ownership or on the capacity for pleasure and pain, but on our rational and autonomous nature; this lets him move from respect for persons to a demanding account of freedom.
- He then tightens the notion of freedom by contrasting autonomy with heteronomy. The Sprite slogan and the billiard-ball example are not ornaments; they are the lecture’s way of showing that action driven by appetite or causal necessity is not yet free in Kant’s sense.
- From there Sandel makes the crucial transition from freedom to morality. If autonomy means self-legislation, then moral worth cannot come from outcomes; it must come from motive, and specifically from acting out of duty rather than inclination.
- He develops this claim experimentally through cases: the shopkeeper, the duty to preserve oneself, the Better Business Bureau slogan, and the honor-code discount scheme. Each example isolates the same question by stripping away consequences and asking what motive is actually doing the moral work.
- The lecture then becomes dialectical. Students press Kant on mixed motives and on the apparent subjectivity of self-given law, and Sandel answers by distinguishing supporting sentiment from grounding reason, then by introducing reverence for the moral law and the idea of one shared practical reason rather than many private moral laws.
- Around the middle of the transcript Sandel performs an internal reset. He explicitly returns to Kant, names the two big questions again, and reorganizes the lecture around three linked contrasts; this recap should be preserved, not flattened away, because it is how he gathers the earlier material into a single formal scheme.
- The final movement turns from preparation to formulation. Sandel introduces hypothetical and categorical imperatives, uses the quotation slide to crystallize the contrast, then moves to the formula of universal law, answers the Mill-style objection that universalization smuggles in consequences, and closes with the humanity formula, applying it to false promise, murder, suicide, and ordinary social interaction before leaving the final verdict open.

## Section Outline
1. **Why Kant, and Why Now.** Sandel introduces Kant as the hardest thinker in the course, explains why the difficulty is worth enduring, and frames the lecture around dignity, morality, and freedom rather than around self-ownership or utility.

2. **Rational Beings, Dignity, and Freedom Beyond Desire.** This section should track the move from Kant’s anti-utilitarian picture of the person to the claim that freedom is not doing what we want but acting autonomously; a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: If satisfying my desires feels free, why does Kant say it is a form of unfreedom?`

3. **Autonomy, Heteronomy, and the Moral Meaning of Freedom.** Use the Sprite slogan, the billiard ball, and the contrast with natural necessity to show how Sandel builds the chain from reason to self-legislation to freedom, and from appetite to heteronomy to unfreedom.

4. **Moral Worth, Good Will, and the Motive of Duty.** Sandel now asks what gives an action moral worth and answers that consequences do not; only the motive of duty does. This section should introduce the good will and the distinction between acting in conformity with duty and acting for the sake of duty.

5. **Examples That Isolate Motive.** Follow the shopkeeper, suicide, Better Business Bureau, and honor-code examples in order to preserve how Sandel tests Kant’s principle by case analysis; a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: Can an action still have moral worth if duty is accompanied by self-interest, sentiment, or the desire to be moral?`

6. **One Moral Law or Many? Shared Reason and the Internal Reset.** Preserve the student challenge about subjectivity and Sandel’s answer that practical reason is shared rather than idiosyncratic, then keep the explicit recap in which he restates the two big questions and reorders the material into three contrasts; a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: If the moral law is self-given, why is morality not merely subjective?`

7. **Three Contrasts and the Imperative Distinction.** Present the triad duty/inclination, autonomy/heteronomy, and hypothetical/categorical imperative, using the extracted slide as visual evidence for the third contrast. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: Does Kant’s universalization test secretly rely on consequences after all?`

8. **Universal Law, Humanity as End, and the Reach of Respect.** Move from the universal-law formula to the humanity formula, then apply them to false promise, murder, suicide, and Patrick’s objection about ordinary social life; a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: How can we use other people as means in everyday life without treating them merely as means?`
